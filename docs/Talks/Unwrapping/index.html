A short talk

Unwrapping an
indiscrete group

three matrices, a transcendental number, and a tree of hyperbolic spaces

Nic Brody · UC Santa Cruz

"I want to show you a subgroup of PSL₂(ℂ) that looks hopeless from the point of view of hyperbolic geometry, and then a different geometry in which it becomes transparent."

Three matrices

\[ x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\omega & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad y_\pi = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \omega\pi & \omega\pi^2 \\ -\omega & 1 + \omega\pi \end{pmatrix} \]

\(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}\), and \(y_\pi = u\,y\,u^{-1}\) for the translation \(u = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \pi \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \(z \mapsto z + \pi\), which commutes with \(x\).

\(\langle x, y \rangle = \Gamma_8\) is the figure-eight knot group (Riley): a lattice in \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb C)\), the holonomy of the hyperbolic structure on \(S^3 \smallsetminus 4_1\).

\(\Gamma = \langle x, y, y_\pi \rangle\) is two copies of \(\Gamma_8\), slid apart by \(\pi\) along the cusp. What group is it? Can we write down a presentation?

"Here are three matrices. x and y are Riley's generators for the figure-eight knot group: two parabolics, meridians of the knot. The third, y_π, is y conjugated by the translation z ↦ z + π. That translation commutes with x, so conjugating the whole knot group by it gives a second copy of Γ₈ that shares x with the first."

[click] "⟨x, y⟩ is a lattice: the quotient of ℍ³ is the figure-eight knot complement."

[click] "So Γ is generated by two copies of the knot group, slid apart along the cusp by π. What is this group? For a Kleinian group, the standard answer comes from geometry. Let's ask the computer."

In hyperbolic space

\(\Gamma_8 = \langle x, y\rangle\): a Dirichlet domain, a finite-volume polyhedron with two ideal vertices. Its face pairings and edge cycles are a presentation of \(\Gamma_8\) (Poincaré's polyhedron theorem).

The orbit of a point under \(\Gamma_8\): one point in each copy of the domain. The points stay apart, spreading out toward the sphere at infinity.

Now add \(y_\pi\). The orbit piles up, and there is no domain to find: poincare comes back with

\(\Gamma\) is not discrete. No fundamental domain, no Poincaré theorem: nothing in \(\mathbb H^3\) hands us a presentation.

[click] "Its orbit: discrete, the points stay apart."

[click] "Now add y_π. The domain computation collapses, and the program reports a word in the generators that is elliptic with an irrational rotation angle: its powers accumulate at the identity. Γ is indiscrete. There's no polyhedron, and so no presentation from ℍ³."

But \(\pi\) is transcendental

Evaluation \(t \mapsto \pi\) is injective on \(\mathbb Q(\omega)(t)\), so nothing is lost by putting the variable back:

\[ \Gamma \;\cong\; \Gamma_t = \langle x,\ y,\ y_t \rangle \subset \mathrm{PGL}_2\big(\mathbb Q(\omega)(t)\big) \]

where \(y_t = u_t\, y\, u_t^{-1}\) for \(u_t = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\): the same matrices, with \(t\) in place of \(\pi\).

A tree. \(t\) has a valuation, at \(t = \infty\). It has a Bruhat–Tits tree, on which \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q(\omega)(t))\) acts.

Of hyperbolic spaces. The residue field is \(\mathbb Q(\omega) \subset \mathbb C\), so the link of a vertex is \(\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Q(\omega))\), the cusps of an \(\mathbb H^3\). Put an \(\mathbb H^3\) at every vertex, its neighbours hanging off its cusps.

Back to one. Setting \(t = \pi\) lays every vertex's \(\mathbb H^3\) onto one, by its frame \(g_v(\pi)\). Run backwards, one \(\mathbb H^3\) unwraps into the tree.

"Here's the trick. π is transcendental over ℚ(ω), so evaluating at π is an isomorphism from ℚ(ω)(t) onto its image. The group Γ is literally the same as the group Γ_t of matrices over the rational function field, with t in place of π."

[click] "And ℚ(ω)(t) has a discrete valuation, at t = ∞: the degree in t. So PGL₂ of it acts on a Bruhat–Tits tree."

[click] "The residue field of that valuation is ℚ(ω), which sits in ℂ. So the link of each vertex is the projective line over ℚ(ω), which is exactly the set of cusps of ℍ³ for the Eisenstein integers. Each vertex carries a copy of ℍ³, and its neighbours hang off its cusps."

[click] "Specializing t to π collapses all these copies onto one. Let's watch that backwards."

Unwrapping

\(t = \pi\): one \(\mathbb H^3\). Each colour is the circle pattern of another vertex of the tree, laid on it by \(g_v(\pi)\); \(\Gamma\) acts here by \(x\), \(y\), \(y_\pi\), indiscretely.

Let \(t\) run from \(\pi\) back to \(\infty\): the one \(\mathbb H^3\) unwraps into the tree of \(\mathbb H^3\)'s, each ball hanging off a cusp of its parent.

\(x\) and \(y\) fix the base ball \(v_0\) and act on it as \(\Gamma_8\), carrying the rest of the tree along.

\(y_t\) fixes the ball \(v_0 \to \infty \to -1\), two steps away, and turns the tree about it. Between the two fixed balls sits the ball at \(\infty\).

[click] "Now let t go from π back to infinity." (about 2.5 s) "The single ℍ³ unwraps into a tree of them."

[click] "x and then y: both fix the base ball and act on it by the figure-eight group."

[click] "y_t, from the identity: it fixes a ball two steps out, through the ball at ∞, and turns the tree about it."

Reading off the group

\(x, y\) fix \(v_0\), \(y_t\) fixes \(u_t v_0\), and between them is the ball at \(\infty\). In either copy of \(\Gamma_8\), the elements fixing that ball are exactly the cusp group \(P = \langle x, \ell \rangle \cong \mathbb Z^2\), so reduced words never backtrack (Bass–Serre):

\[ \Gamma \;\cong\; \Gamma_8 *_P \Gamma_8 \;=\; \big\langle\, x, y, y' \;\big|\; wx = yw,\ \ w'x = y'w',\ \ \ell = \ell' \,\big\rangle \]

\(w = x^{-1}yxy^{-1}\), the longitude \(\ell = yx^{-1}y^{-1}x^2y^{-1}x^{-1}y\) (\(z \mapsto z + 2\sqrt{-3}\)), and \(w', \ell'\) are the same words in \(x, y'\).

That is \(\pi_1\) of the double of the figure-eight knot complement: a closed 3-manifold with an incompressible torus, so no Kleinian group is isomorphic to it. Containing a lattice with infinite index, \(\Gamma\) is dense.

"Now we can just read it off. x and y fix the base vertex, y_t fixes the vertex u_t v₀, and the path between them goes through the ball at ∞. The elements of the knot group fixing that ball are the elements fixing the cusp ∞, which is the peripheral ℤ², meridian and longitude, and the same for the conjugate copy. So an alternating word never backtracks: Bass–Serre theory, or ping-pong on a tree, gives the amalgam."

"The presentation: the Riley relation for each copy, and the longitudes identified. The longitude is the word ℓ, translation by 2√−3: that's the cusp shape."

[click] "And we recognise it: π₁ of the double of the knot complement along its boundary torus. That's a closed Haken manifold that isn't hyperbolic, so this group can't be Kleinian. Since Γ contains the lattice Γ₈ with infinite index, it's dense in PSL₂(ℂ), as the first picture suggested."

A transcendental twist

Let \(F\) be a number field, \(A, B \subset \mathrm{PGL}_2(F)\) share a subgroup \(C\), and \(s \in \mathrm{PGL}_2(F(t))\) centralize \(C\). If the elements of \(A\) fixing the first edge of \([v_0, s v_0]\), and those of \(sBs^{-1}\) fixing its last edge, are exactly \(C\), then

\[ \langle A,\ sBs^{-1} \rangle \;\cong\; A *_C B, \]

and so is its value at any transcendental \(t \in \mathbb C\).

The residue field contains \(F\), so the link of \(v_0\) sees \(A\) faithfully: an edge stabilizer is the stabilizer of a cusp. (Twisting by a prime \(p\) instead, it would be a congruence subgroup.)

\(s\) must centralize \(C\), so \(C\) is elementary: slide along a cusp, \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), or dilate along an axis, \(\mathrm{diag}(t, 1)\).

P. Shalen, Linear representations of certain amalgamated products (1979).

"This is Peter Shalen's trick for making amalgams linear. Conjugate one factor by something with a transcendental entry that centralizes the amalgamated subgroup. The tree of the valuation at t does the rest."

"The key point is that the residue field contains the field of definition, so reduction is injective on A and B. If you twisted by an integer p instead, you'd be in the p-adic tree and the edge stabilizer would be a congruence subgroup: that's how you get SL₂(ℤ[1/p]) as an amalgam over Γ₀(p), not over C."

[click] "Since s has to commute with C, C has to be elementary: a cusp group, which is our example, or the stabilizer of an axis."
