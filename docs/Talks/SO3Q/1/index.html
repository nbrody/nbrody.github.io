\(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb{Q})\)

One dimension down

Nic Brody · UC Santa Cruz

First of three talks on SO₃(ℚ). Behind the title: the rational points of the unit circle, each with a spike of height 1/denominator — we will meet this picture again. Keys: S = speaker view, Esc = overview, F = fullscreen. Present = phone remote (QR code).

One of my favorite groups

\[ \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q) \;=\; \big\{\, A \in \mathrm{M}_3(\mathbb Q) \;:\; A^{\mathsf T}\!A = I,\ \ \det A = 1 \,\big\} \]

the rotations of space whose matrices have rational entries — for instance

\[ \frac13\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \]
"SO_3(Q) is one of my favorite groups." The example is the rotation by 60° about the axis (1, 1, 1): trace 2 = 1 + 2cos θ, and A − Aᵀ points along (1, 1, 1).

I'd like to describe how geometric, arithmetic, and topological properties make this a beautiful object to study.

"I'd like to describe how geometric, arithmetic, and topological properties make this a beautiful object to study." Each word lights up on a click.

One dimension down

\[ \mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q) \;=\; \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} x & -y \\ y & x \end{pmatrix} \;:\; x, y \in \mathbb Q,\ \ x^2 + y^2 = 1 \right\} \]

the rotations of the plane with rational entries.

"We'll begin by studying the group one dimension down."
Visualization (3 clicks): the grid → a rotated copy that only lands back on the grid at quarter turns → the unit circle. "We can consider rotations of the plane that send the standard square grid back to itself. This is a very constrained set of symmetries. Notice that the length-1 vectors need to have only 4 options for where they can go, and once the first two are determined, the whole thing is decided." A finite group

\[ \mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Z) \;=\; \{\, I,\ J,\ J^2,\ J^3 \,\} \;\cong\; \mathbb Z/4, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

But what if we only asked that one-third of the grid points land on grid points? Or one-fifth?

"So SO2Z is a finite group. However, now we can ask the question: what if we only require that, say, one-third of the points mapped to other points, or one-fifth of the points mapped to grid points?"
Visualization (5 states): a generic rotation (24°): only the origin lands → the two index-5 sub-grids → R = rotation by arccos(3/5) carries indigo onto pink → R² (index 25) → R³ (index 125). "Note, this turns into a question about sending some sub grid to another sub grid. Suddenly, there are way more possibilities! Consider the rotation by arccosine of 3/5. This maps a finite index subgroup back to another finite index subgroup, and iterating it does the same." Facts: indigo = (2 − i)ℤ[i], spanned by (2, −1), (1, 2); pink = (2 + i)ℤ[i]. Rⁿ carries (2 − i)ⁿℤ[i] onto (2 + i)ⁿℤ[i], index 5ⁿ. R has infinite order (Niven: the only rational cosines of rational multiples of π are 0, ±1/2, ±1). Crystallographers call these coincidence site lattices — the Σ5 grain boundary is exactly this rotation.

Rotations with rational entries

\[ \begin{pmatrix} x & -y \\ y & x \end{pmatrix} = \frac1c \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \]

Rational rotations are Pythagorean triples:

\(3^2+4^2=5^2\)53.13°
\(5^2+12^2=13^2\)67.38°
\(8^2+15^2=17^2\)61.93°
\(7^2+24^2=25^2\)73.74°
\(20^2+21^2=29^2\)46.40°
\(12^2+35^2=37^2\)71.08°
\(9^2+40^2=41^2\)77.32°
\(33^2+56^2=65^2\)59.49°

In lowest terms, the rotation sends exactly \(\tfrac1c\) of the grid to the grid.

"Now we can ask: what are the possible 2x2 rotation matrices with rational entries? Turns out this is equivalent to having a Pythagorean triple: a² + b² = c²." The angle under each triple is arccos(a/c). The 1/c fact: the rotation (a + bi)/c = β/β̄ carries exactly the sub-grid β̄ℤ[i] (index N(β) = c) onto βℤ[i].

Which denominators occur?

The denominators \(c \le 100\): 1, 5, 13, 17, 25, 29, 37, 41, 53, 61, 65, 73, 85, 89, 97.

primes \(\equiv 1\), primes \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\). A denominator \(c\) occurs \(\iff\) every prime factor of \(c\) is \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\).

So one-third is impossible. Apart from the quarter turns, the most any rotation can do is one-fifth.

"It turns out that we can only do this when the denominator's prime factorization only has primes which are 1 modulo 4." Why: if a prime q divides c then a² ≡ −b² (mod q) with q ∤ b (primitive), so −1 is a square mod q, i.e. q ≡ 1 (mod 4); and c is odd since a² + b² ≡ 2 (mod 4) when a, b are both odd. Conversely every such c occurs (products of the generators coming up).

Unravelling \(\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q)\)

Write the rotation \(\tfrac1c\left(\begin{smallmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{smallmatrix}\right)\) as \(\tfrac{a+bi}{c} \in \mathbb C\), so composing rotations is multiplying. For each prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), write \(p = x^2 + y^2\) and let \(r_p = \tfrac{(x+iy)^2}{p}\):

prime \(p\)\(x^2 + y^2\)generator \(r_p\)angle
5\(2^2+1^2\)\(\tfrac{3+4i}{5}\)53.13°
13\(3^2+2^2\)\(\tfrac{5+12i}{13}\)67.38°
17\(4^2+1^2\)\(\tfrac{15+8i}{17}\)28.07°
29\(5^2+2^2\)\(\tfrac{21+20i}{29}\)43.60°
\(\vdots\)\(\vdots\)\(\vdots\)\(\vdots\)
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q) \;&=\; \mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Z) \times \langle r_5\rangle \times \langle r_{13}\rangle \times \langle r_{17}\rangle \times \langle r_{29}\rangle \times \cdots \\ &\cong\; \mathbb Z/4 \,\times\, \textstyle\bigoplus_{p \,\equiv\, 1\ (4)} \mathbb Z \end{aligned} \]
"We can unravel the structure of SO2Q as consisting of SO2Z, and then for each prime which is 1 modulo 4, we have a generating rotation." Fermat: p ≡ 1 (mod 4) is a sum of two squares, uniquely (x > y > 0). The generator r_p is the rotation whose Pythagorean triple is Euclid's (x² − y², 2xy, x² + y²). Every rotation is uniquely ±iᵏ ∏ r_p^(n_p), with denominator ∏ p^|n_p|.

This leads to a pretty striking observation.

The obvious geometry of \(\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q)\) is the set of rational points on a circle …

… but there is a more intrinsic geometry, one that reflects the algebraic structure of the group.

"This leads us to a pretty striking observation. Although the obvious geometry of SO2(Q) consists of rational points on a circle, there's a more intrinsic geometry that reflects the algebraic structure of this group."
Visualization (8 states): the circle (spike height 1/c) → powers of r₅ → unwrap to ℤ → ⟨r₅, r₁₃⟩ on the circle (a tangle of multiplication chords) → it flies into ℤ² → denominators: log c = |a| log 5 + |b| log 13, a taxicab distance (diamonds) → r₁₇ adds a third dimension (auto-rotates; remote joystick orbits, Play/Pause stops the spin) → one direction per prime ≡ 1 mod 4. Hover any dot for its exact value, denominator and factorization.

And this geometry is that of an

infinite-dimensional lattice!

\[ \mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q)\,/\,\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Z) \;\cong\; \bigoplus_{p \,\equiv\, 1\ (4)} \mathbb Z \] \[ \pm\, i^k \prod_p r_p^{\,n_p} \;\longmapsto\; (n_5,\, n_{13},\, n_{17},\, n_{29},\, \dots) \]
"And this geometry is that of an infinite-dimensional lattice!!" The coordinate n_p is the π_p-adic valuation of the rotation, viewed in ℚ(i). The denominator is ∏ p^|n_p|, so log(denominator) is a weighted ℓ¹ norm on the lattice.

Appendix

Why primes \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\)?

  • A Gaussian integer \(\pi = x+iy\) gives the rotation \(\pi/\bar\pi = \frac{(x^2-y^2) + 2xy\,i}{x^2+y^2}\) — Euclid's formula for Pythagorean triples — and every rational rotation arises this way.
  • If a prime \(q\) divides the denominator \(c\) of \(\frac{a+bi}{c}\) in lowest terms, then \(a^2 \equiv -b^2 \pmod q\) with \(q \nmid b\), so \(-1\) is a square mod \(q\): \(q \equiv 1 \pmod 4\).
  • For \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(p = \pi_p \bar\pi_p\) in \(\mathbb Z[i]\), and \(r_p = \pi_p/\bar\pi_p\). Unique factorization in \(\mathbb Z[i]\) makes every rotation \(\pm i^k \prod r_p^{\,n_p}\) uniquely, with denominator \(\prod p^{|n_p|}\).
  • \(\frac{a+bi}{c} = \beta/\bar\beta\) sends the sub-grid \(\bar\beta\,\mathbb Z[i]\) of index \(c\) onto \(\beta\,\mathbb Z[i]\) — exactly the points that land on the grid.
