Second of three talks on SO₃(ℚ). Behind the title: rational points of the sphere, (a/d, b/d, c/d) with a² + b² + c² = d², bigger dots for smaller d. Keys: S = speaker view, Esc = overview, F = fullscreen. Present = phone remote (QR code).

Last time: \(\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q)\)

  • A rational rotation of the plane is a Pythagorean triple \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\), i.e. \(\frac{a+bi}{c} = \frac{\pi}{\bar\pi}\) for a Gaussian integer \(\pi\).
  • Only primes \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) appear in denominators, and each contributes one generating rotation \(r_p\).
  • Unique factorization in \(\mathbb Z[i]\): \(\ \mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q) \cong \mathbb Z/4 \times \bigoplus_{p \equiv 1\,(4)} \mathbb Z\): an infinite-dimensional lattice, a product of lines.
Quick recap of part 1. Denominators of rational rotations of the plane are products of primes ≡ 1 mod 4; for each such p the rotations with denominator pⁿ are the powers of one r_p (up to quarter turns); so SO₂(ℚ)/SO₂(ℤ) ≅ ⊕ ℤ, a product of lines.
"Now let's go up to three dimensions. Again, SO3Z is finite." Finite, then infinite

\[ \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z) = \{ \text{signed permutation matrices of det } 1 \} \;\cong\; S_4 \]

But \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) is infinite, e.g. \(\ \frac13\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1&2&2\\ 2&1&-2\\ -2&2&-1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\) has infinite order. Each column is a rational point of the sphere: \(a^2+b^2+c^2 = d^2\).

How do we find them all? In the plane we used Gaussian integers. In space: quaternions.

The 24 = S₄ via the action on the four body diagonals of the cube. (1/3)(1,2,2; 2,1,−2; −2,2,−1) has trace 1/3 = 1 + 2cos θ, so cos θ = −1/3 — not 0, ±½, ±1 — and it has infinite order. It is R_{1+i+j} (coming up).

Quaternions

\[ \mathbb H = \{\, a + bi + cj + dk \,\}, \qquad i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1 \]
  • Conjugate \(\bar q = a - bi - cj - dk\) and norm \(N(q) = q\bar q = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2\), so \(q^{-1} = \bar q / N(q)\).
  • \(N(qr) = N(q)\,N(r)\): it is somewhat miraculous that we can take two solutions of \(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2 = 1\) and produce another! (In the plane: \((ax-by)^2 + (ay+bx)^2 = 1\).)
  • Not commutative: \(ij = k = -ji\).
Hamilton, 1843 (carved into Broom Bridge). N(qr) = N(q)N(r) is Euler's four-square identity.

Conjugation fixes the real part

For \(q \ne 0\), consider \(\ v \mapsto q\,v\,q^{-1}\ \) on all of \(\mathbb H\).

  • It respects the algebra, \(q(vw)q^{-1} = (qvq^{-1})(qwq^{-1})\), and preserves norms: \(N(qvq^{-1}) = N(v)\).
  • It fixes the real part: \(\ \mathrm{Re}(q v q^{-1}) = \mathrm{Re}(v)\), since \(\mathrm{Re}(xy) = x_0 y_0 - \vec x \cdot \vec y = \mathrm{Re}(yx)\).
  • So it preserves the pure quaternions \(\{bi + cj + dk\} = \mathbb R^3\) — and \(\mathbb Q^3\) when \(q\) is rational — acting by a rotation \(R_q\).
"Describe how we can use integer quaternions to determine rational rotations: first describing how quaternion conjugation acts on quaternions, and noting that it fixes the real part; hence we get an action on ℚ³." Orthogonal because it preserves N; determinant +1 because q ↦ det R_q is continuous on the connected unit quaternions and R_1 = I.
Visualization (3 states): ℍ = ℝ·1 ⊕ ℝ³ — the gauge on the left is the real line, the sphere is the pure part; the sample quaternion v = t + w → conjugation by q = 1 + i + j: the real part stays put (FIXED), the pure part w and the whole sphere (and the die) turn → the axis (1, 1, 0) and the angle 2 arccos(1/√3) ≈ 109.47°. Dictionary: a + bi + cj + dk ↔ rotation about (b, c, d) by 2 arccos(a/√N).

Integer quaternions, rational rotations

\[ R_q = \frac{1}{N(q)} \begin{pmatrix} a^2+b^2-c^2-d^2 & 2(bc-ad) & 2(bd+ac) \\ 2(bc+ad) & a^2-b^2+c^2-d^2 & 2(cd-ab) \\ 2(bd-ac) & 2(cd+ab) & a^2-b^2-c^2+d^2 \end{pmatrix} \]
  • \(q = 1+i+j\) gives \(\frac13\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1&2&2\\ 2&1&-2\\ -2&2&-1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\); \(\ q = 3+i+j+k\) gives last time’s \(\frac13\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2&-1&2\\ 2&2&-1\\ -1&2&2 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\).
  • \(q = a + bi\) is a Gaussian integer, and \(R_q\) is last time’s rotation \(\frac{a+bi}{a-bi}\), now about the \(i\)-axis.
  • Every rational rotation arises, from a primitive integer quaternion unique up to sign: our first little dictionary. The denominator of \(R_q\) is the odd part of \(N(q)\).
The Euler–Rodrigues formula. Since R_{λq} = R_q, any rational quaternion can be scaled to a primitive integer one. Denominators are always odd (a² + b² + c² ≡ 0 mod 4 forces all even), and every odd number occurs. Fun fact: R_{1+i+j} sends exactly one-third of ℤ³ to ℤ³ — impossible in the plane (last time), and the Σ3 twin boundary of cubic crystals.
Visualization: R_{1+i+j} with the images of i, j, k and their exact coordinates → explorer: step a, b, c, d or click a preset (1 + i and 1 + i + j + k land in SO₃(ℤ); 2 + i is last time's r₅; 1 + 2i + 2j + 2k has denominator 13). Drag to orbit.

Counting: Jacobi

The plane

\(r_2(n) = \#\{\, n = x^2 + y^2 \,\}\)

\(r_2(n) = 4 \sum_{d \mid n} \chi_{-4}(d)\)

\(r_2(p) = 8\) if \(p \equiv 1\), \(\ 0\) if \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\)

4 units \(\pm 1, \pm i\): 2 primes of norm \(p\), namely \(\pi, \bar\pi\)

Space

\(r_4(n) = \#\{\, n = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 \,\}\)

\(r_4(n) = 8 \sum_{d \mid n,\ 4 \nmid d} d\)

\(r_4(p) = 8(p+1)\) for every odd prime \(p\)

8 units \(\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\): \(p+1\) primes of norm \(p\)

"Discuss Jacobi's theorem" — Thm (Jacobi): the number of representations of an odd prime p as a sum of four squares is 8(p+1). General formula r₄(n) = 8 Σ_{d | n, 4 ∤ d} d. Plane analogue (Fermat/Jacobi two-square theorem): r₂(n) = 4 Σ_{d|n} χ₋₄(d). So in the plane each p ≡ 1 mod 4 gives 2 primes up to units (a line: r_p and r_p⁻¹); in space every odd p gives p + 1 primes up to units.

The \(p + 1\) primes of norm \(p\)

Up to \(\pm1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\), take \(\mathrm{Re}(q) > 0\) and \(q \equiv 1\) (mod 2) if \(p \equiv 1\), \(\ q \equiv 1 + i + j\) (mod 2) if \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\). Call this set \(A_p\).

\(A_5 = \{1 \pm 2i,\ 1 \pm 2j,\ 1 \pm 2k\}\): \(R_{1 \pm 2i}\) turn about the \(i\)-axis — they live in last time’s \(\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q)\) — but the other four turn about \(j\) and \(k\).

Interactive: pick p. The chips are A_p (counted by brute force: 8(p+1) quaternions of norm p, divided by the 8 units); the little sphere shows the rotation axes of the R_π (a pair π, π̄ gives an axis ±u). A₃ = {1 ± i ± j}, A₁₃ = {3 ± 2i, 3 ± 2j, 3 ± 2k, 1 ± 2i ± 2j ± 2k}. Each A_p is closed under π ↦ π̄ = π⁻¹ (up to scalars).

Factoring: Hurwitz

Fundamental theorem of quaternion arithmetic

Let \(q\) be a primitive integer quaternion. If \(p\) is an odd prime dividing \(N(q)\), there is a unique quaternion of norm \(p\) (up to associates) which left-divides \(q\).

  • Hurwitz: allow \(a, b, c, d\) all in \(\mathbb Z + \tfrac12\) too. That ring has 24 units and a Euclidean algorithm.
  • So \(q = \pi_1 \pi_2 \cdots \pi_n\) with \(N(\pi_i) = p_i\), for any ordering of the primes of \(N(q)\), uniquely up to units.
  • In the plane this was unique factorization in \(\mathbb Z[i]\), which gave \(\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q) \cong \mathbb Z/4 \times \bigoplus \mathbb Z\).
"…and then Hurwitz's theorem. Contextualize these as higher-dimensional analogues of the SO_2(Q) stuff." The Hurwitz order: 24 units (±1, ±i, ±j, ±k, (±1 ± i ± j ± k)/2) — the vertices of the 24-cell; as rotations they give the 12 rotations of a tetrahedron. Euclidean ⇒ PID ⇒ left divisors of norm p are unique up to units. For odd primes the statement descends to integer quaternions.
"Consequence: for an odd prime p, we can label the vertices of an infinite tree with quaternions of norm pⁿ." Visualization (5 states): rotations with denominator 5ⁿ applied to one point of the sphere — a dense tangle → the 6-regular tree; each edge points along the rotation axis of its prime (1 ± 2i along i, …), labels are the products, e.g. (1 + 2i)² = −3 + 4i → the powers of 1 + 2i are the i-axis of the tree: last time's line ℤ is one geodesic → p = 3: a planar H-tree, labels 1 ± i ± j, … → p = 13: 14 directions. ⟨A_p⟩ is free of rank (p+1)/2; SO₃(ℤ[1/p]) acts on T_{p+1} with vertex stabilizer SO₃(ℤ). Hover any vertex for its word, quaternion and denominator.

The line is the 2-regular tree. The question: how do the trees for different primes fit together? In the plane, generators for different primes commuted. In space they don't — but they metacommute.

Metacommutation

Primes of different norms don’t commute: \((1+i+j)(1+2i) \ne (1+2i)(1+i+j)\). But:

Hurwitz, again

For \(\pi \in A_p\) and \(\sigma \in A_q\) (\(p \ne q\)), \(\pi\sigma\) has a unique left divisor in \(A_q\): \(\ \pi\sigma = \pm\,\sigma'\pi'\ \) for unique \(\sigma' \in A_q\), \(\pi' \in A_p\).

\[ \begin{aligned} (1+i+j)(1+2i) &= -1+3i+j-2k \\ &= -\,(1+2k)(1-i+j) \end{aligned} \]

Fixing \(\sigma\), \(\pi \mapsto \pi'\) permutes the \(p+1\) primes of norm \(p\) — like a Möbius transformation of \(\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_p)\) — and its sign is the Legendre symbol \(\big(\tfrac qp\big)\) (Cohn–Kumar).

"Describe metacommutation of Hurwitz primes." Apply Hurwitz to πσ (norm pq): its unique left divisor of norm q, normalized into A_q, is σ'; the cofactor is automatically ±π' with π' ∈ A_p (the congruence conditions mod 2 are preserved). Cohn & Kumar, "Metacommutation of Hurwitz primes" (Proc. AMS 2015): the permutation's sign is (q/p); its number of fixed points is 1 + ((t² − 4q)/p) with t = 2 Re(σ) — like σ acting on ℙ¹(𝔽_p). Checked here by computer for many (p, q).
Visualization (2 states): the square (1+i+j)(1+2i) = −(1+2k)(1−i+j) → a staircase word (1+i+j)(1+2i)(1+i+j)(1+2j)(1+i−j)(1+2k) sorted by six metacommutations into −(1+2k)(1+2i)(1+2k)·(1−i+j)(1−i+j)(1+i+j). Every rotation with denominator 3ᵃ5ᵇ is uniquely a reduced 5-word times a reduced 3-word (times an element of SO₃(ℤ)): "a square-like calculator". Remote Reset replays it.

\(\mathrm{SO}_3\big(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1{65}]\big)\)

Primes of norm 5 and 13: one vertex, \(3 + 7\) edges (a prime and its inverse), and one square for each metacommutation: \(\frac{6 \cdot 14}{4} = 21\) squares. Its universal cover is \(T_6 \times T_{14}\).

Interactive: click a square to see its relation. The three gold squares commute: (1+2i)(3+2i) = (3+2i)(1+2i) = −1 + 8i, and likewise about j and k — rotations about one axis. They tile periodic flats: last time's lattice ⟨r₅, r₁₃⟩ ≅ ℤ² sits inside T₆ × T₁₄. Most flats are aperiodic. (21 squares = 84 metacommutation relations, 4 corners per square.)
"…and how this builds an infinite-dimensional product of trees." Visualization (3 states): T₄ × T₆ (balls, projected from 4D; copies of each tree through every vertex of the other, squares in between) with the sorted grid as a gold flat → three primes 3, 5, 7: a cube whose six faces are metacommutation squares (all six orders of π σ τ agree) → one more tree direction T_{p+1} for every odd prime. So \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) acts on an

infinite-dimensional product of trees

\[ X \;=\; \prod\nolimits'_{p\ \text{odd}} T_{p+1} \]
  • Vertex stabilizers are copies of \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z)\): finite.
  • \(\Gamma = \big\langle \bigcup_p A_p \big\rangle\) has index 24 and acts simply transitively on the vertices, with presentation \(\ \big\langle \bigcup_p A_p \;\big|\; \pi\sigma = \pm\sigma'\pi' \big\rangle\).
  • Last time’s infinite-dimensional lattice is a flat inside \(X\).
∏′ = restricted product: all but finitely many coordinates at the base vertex. SO₃(ℚ)/SO₃(ℤ) ↔ vertices of X. Checked by computer: exactly 576 = 24·4·6 rotations have denominator 15, and they are exactly (w₅·w₃)·SO₃(ℤ) with |w₅| = |w₃| = 1. SO₂(ℚ) (rotations about the i-axis) moves along an axis of T_{p+1} for p ≡ 1 mod 4 and fixes a vertex for p ≡ 3 mod 4: so ⊕ℤ is a flat in X.

Appendix

Where the trees come from

  • For odd \(p\), \(\mathbb H \otimes \mathbb Q_p \cong M_2(\mathbb Q_p)\), so \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q_p) \cong \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q_p)\), which acts on its Bruhat–Tits tree \(T_{p+1}\), whose vertices neighbouring the base are \(\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_p)\).
  • At \(p = 2\) and \(\infty\) the quaternions stay a division algebra, so \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q_2)\) and \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb R)\) are compact: no tree there (Hilbert reciprocity).
  • \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) is a lattice in \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb A)\), and \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\) is a lattice in \(\prod_{p \mid n,\ p\ \text{odd}} T_{p+1}\).
  • The 24 Hurwitz units are the vertices of the 24-cell; as rotations they are the 12 rotations of a tetrahedron.
