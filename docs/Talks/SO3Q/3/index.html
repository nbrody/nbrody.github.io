Last time: \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\)

  • A rational rotation is \(R_q\) for a primitive integer quaternion \(q\), up to sign; its denominator is the odd part of \(N(q)\).
  • For each odd prime \(p\), the rotations with denominator a power of \(p\) form a \((p+1)\)-regular tree \(T_{p+1}\) (Hurwitz: unique factorization).
  • Metacommutation glues the trees: \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) acts on \(\prod'_p T_{p+1}\) with finite stabilizers, and \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\) on the finite product \(\prod_{p \mid n} T_{p+1}\) — a jungle.
Recap of part 2. R_q v = q v q̄ / N(q); every rational rotation arises from a primitive integer q, unique up to sign, and the reduced denominator is the odd part of N(q). The p-power rotations form T_{p+1} (vertices = SO₃(ℤ[1/p]) / SO₃(ℤ)); metacommutation πσ = ±σ′π′ makes the squares of the product. "Jungle" = a locally compact product of trees (Nic's word).

The central question

Let \(A\) be a finite set of rotations of the 2-sphere.
What is the group \(\langle A \rangle\) that it generates?

Over \(\mathbb R\) this is hopeless: Galois conjugates of arithmetic groups put surface groups and hyperbolic 3-manifold groups inside \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb R)\).

Over \(\mathbb Q\) there is a conjectural answer, and it is short.

"Let A be a finite set of rotations of the 2-sphere. What is the group generated by A?" Over ℝ: e.g. the right-angled pentagon group sits in O(x² + y² − √2 z²; ℤ[√2]); the Galois twist √2 ↦ −√2 makes the form definite, so the (surface) group lands in a compact O₃. Likewise arithmetic Kleinian groups whose quaternion algebra is ramified at every real place embed in SO₃(ℝ) = PSU(2). "Classifying subgroups of SO₃(ℝ) appears quite daunting." So: SO₃(ℚ).

Where the question came from

  • Long–Reid: can a hyperbolic surface group act properly on a jungle?
  • A faithful \(\rho : \pi_1 \Sigma \to \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) would do it: stabilizers in \(\prod T_{p+1}\) are finite. (A totally unitary representation.)
  • For \(G = \langle a, b \mid [a,b]^2 \rangle\): \(\ a \mapsto R_{2+3j} = \tfrac1{13}\left(\begin{smallmatrix} -5&0&12\\ 0&13&0\\ -12&0&-5 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(\ b \mapsto R_{1+2i+2j+3k} = \tfrac19\left(\begin{smallmatrix} -4&1&8\\ 7&-4&4\\ 4&8&1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Then \([a,b]\) is a half-turn, so \([a,b]^2 \mapsto 1\).
  • Not faithful: \(a^2 b a^{-1} b^{-1}\) and \(b a b a^{-1} b^{-1} a^{-1} b\) differ in \(G\) but give the same rotation. So: what are the subgroups of \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\)?

Algebra: fix an axis

  • The rotations about a rational axis \(v\) are the \(R_q\) with \(q = a + b\,v\), of norm \(a^2 + |v|^2 b^2\).
  • So they are the norm-one elements of \(K = \mathbb Q(\sqrt{-|v|^2})\): \(\ \cong \mu_K \times \bigoplus_{p \text{ split in } K} \mathbb Z\).
  • \(v = k\): last time’s \(\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Q)\), with \(\mathbb Z/4\) and the primes \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\).  \(v = (1,1,1)\): \(\mathbb Z/6\) and the primes \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 3\).
  • Commuting rotations of infinite order share their axis, so every infinite abelian subgroup of \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) sits (virtually) in one of these.

Geometry: the hidden trees

  • For each odd prime \(p\), all of \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q) \subset \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q_p) \cong \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q_p)\) acts on \(T_{p+1}\), and \(d(o, g\cdot o)\) is the power of \(p\) in the denominator of \(g\).
  • For \(q = a + v\) primitive, \(R_q\) is hyperbolic of translation length \(v_p(N q) - 2\,v_p(a)\) when that is positive; otherwise elliptic, fixing a line if \(p\) splits in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-|v|^2})\) and a bounded set if not. Never parabolic.

Geometry: ping pong and primary groups

Definition

\(\Gamma \le \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) is primary if for some prime \(p\) only finitely many \(g \in \Gamma\) have small powers of \(p\) in their denominators — that is, \(\Gamma\) acts properly on \(T_{p+1}\).

Proposition

A (finitely generated) primary group has a free subgroup of finite index.

A proper action on a tree with finite stabilizers (Bass–Serre). Ping pong makes many: \(\langle A_p \rangle\) is free and simply transitive on \(T_{p+1}\), and so is any pair whose half-trees nest as in the picture.

Nic: "It may also happen that for some prime p, there are only finitely many g ∈ Γ which have small powers of p in their denominators. We'll call such groups primary. Prop: if Γ is primary there is a finite index subgroup F ≤ Γ which is free." Since d(o, g·o) = v_p(den g), "finitely many g with v_p(den) ≤ N for each N" is exactly properness. Stabilizers are finite (compact ∩ discrete), so Γ is the fundamental group of a finite graph of finite groups: virtually free (Karrass–Pietrowski–Solitar).
g = 1 + 2k, h = 1 + 2i. g maps everything outside the half-tree U_{g⁻} into U_{g⁺}, and so on; the orbit of o under ⟨g, h⟩ (white) is the 4-regular subtree of words in 1 ± 2k, 1 ± 2i. Proper, free: primary.

Arithmetic: \(\mathrm{SO}_3(A)\)

  • Every subring of \(\mathbb Q\) is \(A = \mathbb Z[\tfrac1S]\) for a set of primes \(S\). For \(A = \mathbb Z[\tfrac1n]\), \(\mathrm{SO}_3(A)\) acts properly and cocompactly on the jungle \(\prod_{p \mid n} T_{p+1}\): a lattice.
  • One prime: virtually free (primary). Two or more: an irreducible lattice in a product of trees. It contains \(\mathbb Z^2\) (last time’s commuting squares), so it is not virtually free.
  • Add finite-index subgroups and conjugates: everything commensurable with some \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\).
"Arithmetic: Take a subring A of Q, and look at SO_3(A)." Only odd primes matter (denominators are odd). Last time: the index-24 subgroup ⟨∪ A_p⟩ acts simply transitively on the vertices of the product. SO₃(ℚ) commensurates SO₃(ℤ[1/n]), so conjugates are commensurable with it.

Conjecture: that’s all

Conjecture

Let \(\Gamma \le \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) be finitely generated. Then either

  1. \(\Gamma\) is (virtually) abelian: it preserves an axis;
  2. \(\Gamma\) is primary, hence virtually free; or
  3. \(\Gamma\) is commensurable with \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\) for an odd \(n\) with at least two prime factors.

Call \(\Gamma\) full if it is neither abelian nor primary. The conjecture: full groups are \(S\)-arithmetic.

Nic's statement: (i) Γ is abelian, (ii) Γ is primary, (iii) Γ is commensurable with SO₃(ℤ[1/n]) for n odd composite. "Virtually" is needed in (i): O₂(ℤ[1/65]) = SO₂(ℤ[1/65]) ⋊ (a half-turn) is not abelian, not primary (it contains ℤ²), and not a lattice. "Composite" means two distinct primes: SO₃(ℤ[1/p^k]) = SO₃(ℤ[1/p]) is primary.

If the conjecture holds

  • \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) contains no surface group — no one-ended hyperbolic group at all, since the lattices in (iii) contain \(\mathbb Z^2\). The \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) road to Long–Reid’s question is closed.
  • Every finitely generated subgroup is a lattice in a jungle, hence finitely presented: \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) is coherent.
  • But \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1{65}])\) is quasi-isometric to \(F_2 \times F_2\), which is not coherent: coherence is not a geometric property.

Geometric rigidity

Theorem

Let \(\Gamma \le \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Q)\) be full, acting discretely on \(X = \prod_{p \in S} T_{p+1}\). Any geometric representation \(\rho : \Gamma \to \mathrm{Aut}(X)\) — one with the same translation lengths on every tree — extends uniquely to \(G = \prod_{p \in S} \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q_p)\).

  • Chiswell: minimal actions with the same length function are equivariantly isometric, so \(\rho\) is conjugate in \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\) to the standard action.
  • \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q_p)\) is self-normalizing in \(\mathrm{Aut}(T_{p+1})\): \(\mathbb Q_p\) has no automorphisms, and a minimal action cannot be centralized.
  • Strong approximation: \(\Gamma\) is dense in each factor, so the extension is unique.
Nic's slides: translation functions τ_v(g) = d_T(v, g v) (for T = Cay(F_S, S), τ_e is word length); minimal = no proper invariant subtree; Chiswell's theorem; ρ′ is geometric if on every factor its translation length function agrees with ρ's. Lemma: PGL₂ℚ_p ≤ Aut(T_{p+1}) is self-normalizing. Proof of the theorem: by Chiswell ρ can be conjugated to the standard action by an element of Aut(X); by strong approximation Γ acts densely on each factor, so the extension ρ̃ is unique.

Two primes: slices

Proposition

Let \(\Gamma \le \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1{pq}])\) be full. Then \(\Gamma\) has finite index if and only if its slice \(\Gamma \cap \mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1p])\) is finitely generated.

  1. The slice is the stabilizer of \(o\) in \(T_{q+1}\), so it is commensurated by \(\Gamma\); its limit set in \(\partial T_{p+1}\) is \(\Gamma\)-invariant, hence all of it (\(\Gamma\) is dense at \(p\)).
  2. A finitely generated discrete group with full limit set acts cocompactly (its minimal subtree is everything): the slice is a lattice, of finite index in \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1p])\).
  3. Dense at \(q\): \(\Gamma\) has as few orbits on the vertices of \(T_{q+1}\) as its closure, with stabilizers of finite index. So \([\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1{pq}]) : \Gamma] < \infty\).

For two primes the conjecture says: slices of full groups are finitely generated.

A reformulation proposed in this talk (please check). Details: (1) Γ_{o} = Γ ∩ SO₃(ℤ_q) and γΓ_oγ⁻¹ = Γ_{γo} meet in the pointwise stabilizer of [o, γo], of finite index in both (the closure of Γ has compact open stabilizers). Commensurable groups have the same limit set, so Λ(slice) is a nonempty closed Γ-invariant subset of ∂T_{p+1}; Γ̄ ⊇ PSL₂(ℚ_p) acts transitively on ∂T, so Λ = ∂T. (2) Bass–Serre: a finitely generated group acting minimally on a tree has finite quotient graph; the minimal subtree is the hull of the limit set, i.e. T. Two lattices, one inside the other ⇒ finite index. (3) For a vertex w in the Γ̄-orbit of v, {γ ∈ Γ̄ : γv = w} is open, so Γ meets it: Γ-orbits = Γ̄-orbits (at most two, as Γ̄ ⊇ PSL₂(ℚ_q)). Then [G : Γ] = Σ over those orbits of [G_v : Γ_v] < ∞. Local dichotomy used throughout: the closure of a Zariski-dense Γ in PGL₂(ℚ_p) is discrete or open (its Lie algebra is Ad-invariant: 0 or 𝔰𝔩₂).

Coarse maximality

  • \(H \le G\) is coarsely maximal if every intermediate subgroup has finite index over \(H\) or in \(G\); roughly maximal if every finite-index subgroup of \(H\) is.
Question

For \(n = pq\) with \(p \equiv q \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), is the torus \(\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n]) \cong \mathbb Z/4 \times \mathbb Z^2\) roughly maximal in \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\)? (A number-theoretic statement.)

If so, a subgroup containing such a \(\mathbb Z^2\) is abelian or arithmetic; and a counterexample to the conjecture would be (1) a hyperbolic group acting properly on a product of trees, or (2) a non-hyperbolic CAT(0) group with no “poison subgroups”.

Nic: "We might say H ≤ G is coarsely maximal if any intermediate subgroup has finite index in either side, and roughly maximal if any finite index subgroup of H is coarsely maximal. Question: for n odd composite, is SO₂(ℤ[1/n]) ≤ SO₃(ℤ[1/n]) roughly maximal? If so, we can prove: any subgroup of SO₃ℚ containing a ℤ² is abelian or arithmetic." The primes must all be 1 mod 4 (so that the torus has full rank): for n = 15, SO₂(ℤ[1/15]) = SO₂(ℤ[1/5]) ⊂ SO₃(ℤ[1/5]) ⊂ SO₃(ℤ[1/15]), with infinite index at both steps. Likewise one needs the statement for every rank-two torus (every axis), not only k.

Invariant partitions

  • \(A = \mathbb Z[\tfrac1n]\): \(G = \mathrm{SO}_3(A)\) acts transitively on \(S^2(A) = \{x \in A^3 : |x| = 1\}\), with stabilizers \(\cong \mathbb Z/4 \times \mathbb Z^k\), \(k = \#\{p \mid n : p \equiv 1 \ (4)\}\).
  • So \(G\)-invariant partitions of \(S^2(A)\) are the subgroups \(H\) between \(\mathrm{Stab}(e_1)\) and \(G\): the part of \(e_1\) is \(H \cdot e_1\).
  • Coarse maximality \(\iff\) every invariant partition has finite parts, or finitely many parts.
  • Over \(\mathbb R\), a green circle shows that \(\mathrm O_2(\mathbb R)\) is maximal in \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb R)\).

A route to the conjecture

  1. knownReduce. Not Zariski dense ⇒ virtually abelian. At each prime of the denominators, \(\Gamma\) is discrete in \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q_p)\) (primary) or has open closure. So full means dense everywhere, which needs two primes: a higher-rank phenomenon.
  2. NicRigidity. A full group determines its jungle, and finite index means cocompact on it.
  3. newSlices. For two primes: finite index ⇔ the slice is finitely generated. Fold it (Stallings, Todd–Coxeter) towards its congruence closure: this is how every case above closes.
  4. openFlats. Show that a full \(\Gamma\) contains a \(\mathbb Z^2\) of rotations about one axis: a closing lemma for products of trees.
  5. openMaximality. Show rank-two tori are roughly maximal: run the partition argument with equidistribution of rational points (Linnik, Duke; Ellenberg–Michel–Venkatesh) and rigidity of \(\mathbb Z^2\)-actions (Einsiedler–Lindenstrauss) in place of open sets and measure.
  6. openInduct on the number of primes, using slices: they are commensurated, with full limit sets.

Step 5 alone settles every full group containing a flat; with step 4, every two-prime group. A flat-free counterexample would be exotic.

A proposed program, not a proof. Step 1: the proper algebraic subgroups of SO₃ are finite, tori and their normalizers; the p-adic closure of a Zariski-dense Γ has Ad-invariant Lie algebra, 0 or 𝔰𝔩₂. If only one prime appears, Γ sits in the lattice SO₃(ℤ[1/p]) and is primary — so fullness is a higher-rank (S-rank ≥ 2) phenomenon, where one expects rigidity (Margulis, Venkataramana). Step 3 is the proposition two slides back; the congruence closure is computable (strong approximation) and gives the target index, e.g. 32,256 for the squared 5–13 group. Step 4: the analogue of Prasad–Raghunathan's periodic flats for lattices; here Γ is not known to be a lattice. Step 5: in Nic's proof over ℝ, "open set + Lebesgue measure" forces P₀ = S². Over ℤ[1/pq] the green circle becomes a torus orbit (rational points on a circle) and the sweep a union of Hecke orbits; Linnik/Duke/EMV equidistribution of rational points on spheres, and Einsiedler–Lindenstrauss's classification of measures invariant under higher-rank torus actions (a ×p, ×q phenomenon), are the natural replacements. Step 6: slices of full groups are commensurated with full limit sets in the remaining trees. Nic: a counterexample would be a hyperbolic group acting properly on a product of trees, or a CAT(0) group that is not hyperbolic and has no "poison subgroups".

Questions

  • Does \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\) have the congruence subgroup property? (Every index above equals its congruence index.)
  • Is thinness always detected in a simple factor? For \(\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\) this is the conjecture: a thin subgroup should be discrete at some \(p\).
  • Does every hyperbolic 3-manifold contain a nontrivial element of integral trace?
  • Can every hyperbolic \(n\)-manifold, \(n \ge 3\), be built from arithmetic pieces? Or surfaces with algebraic traces?
Nic's closing questions, verbatim in spirit. Thin = Zariski dense of infinite index; "detected in a simple factor" = discrete in one PGL₂(ℚ_p), i.e. primary.

Details

  • For \(g \in \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q_p)\) with trace \(t\) and determinant \(\Delta\), the translation length is \(\max(0,\ v_p(\Delta) - 2v_p(t))\); for \(R_q\), \(t = 2a\), \(\Delta = N(q)\), and the eigenvalues \(a \pm \sqrt{-|v|^2}\) lie in \(\mathbb Q_p\) exactly when \(p\) splits.
  • Local dichotomy: the closure of a Zariski-dense \(\Gamma\) in \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb Q_p)\) has an \(\mathrm{Ad}\)-invariant Lie algebra, so it is \(0\) (discrete) or \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) (open).
  • Computer checks: \(d(o, g\cdot o)\) and the translation length formula (900 random rotations); fixed lines exactly in the split case; transitivity on \(S^2(\mathbb Z[\tfrac1n])\) for \(n = 3, 5, 13, 15, 21, 65\); the representation of \(\langle a, b \mid [a,b]^2\rangle\) is not faithful (3833 elements of length \(\le 7\), 3681 images).
