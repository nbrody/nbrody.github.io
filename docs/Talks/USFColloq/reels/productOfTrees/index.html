What you're seeing

In the blue tree every vertex has 3 edges, and in the violet tree every vertex has 4. Their product has a vertex \((u, v)\) for each pair of vertices, an edge whenever one coordinate moves along an edge while the other stays put, and a square \(e \times f\) for each pair of edges.

You can build it by sliding: copy the blue tree to the white vertex, and slide copies out along every violet edge. Each blue edge sweeps out a square.

Pick a line in each tree. Their product is a flat, a copy of the plane tiled by squares (gold). Every square lies in infinitely many flats, and flats branch off each other along every line of squares, so the product is a two-dimensional Euclidean building.

The squares around a vertex meet a small sphere in the complete bipartite graph \(K_{3,4}\), the link. Its shortest cycles have length \(4\), which is Gromov's condition for a square complex to have no positive curvature, so the product is CAT(0): between any two points there is exactly one straight path.

The tree \(T_{p+1}\) is the Bruhat–Tits tree of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q}_p)\), so \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q}_2) \times \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q}_3)\) acts on \(T_3 \times T_4\). Groups that act on products of trees with a compact quotient include arithmetic groups built from quaternions and the finitely presented simple groups of Burger and Mozes.

The picture is a shadow: each tree is drawn in its own plane, so the product lives in \(\mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}^2 = \mathbb{R}^4\), and we see its linear projection to \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Squares stay parallelograms and flats stay planes.
