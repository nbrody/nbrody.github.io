Ramanujan Graphs

Alexander Lubotzky, Ralph Phillips, Peter Sarnak · Combinatorica 8 (1988), 261–277

Integer quaternions of norm $p$ generate a free group that acts on a tree. Reducing it modulo a second prime $q$ folds the tree into a finite $(p+1)$-regular graph. This paper proves that their nontrivial eigenvalues are as small as a large regular graph's can be, and the proof comes down to counting quaternions.

Context

Received June 25, 1986, revised August 5, 1987, after an announcement at STOC 1986. Refereed and published; not formally verified. The deep input is the Ramanujan conjecture for weight-two cusp forms, which the paper takes from Eichler (1954) and Igusa (1959).

Before the paper background

Expanders and the spectral gap

Take a graph on $n$ vertices in which every vertex has the same degree $k$. Fix $k$, say $k=6$, and let $n$ grow. Such a graph is a good expander if it has no bottleneck: every set $S$ of at most half the vertices has many edges leaving it, in proportion to its size. The Cheeger constant measures the worst case,

$$h(X)=\min_{0\lt |S|\le n/2}\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|},$$

where $\partial S$ is the set of edges with one end in $S$. A family of $k$-regular graphs with $n\to\infty$ and $h$ bounded below is an expander family. Random regular graphs are expanders with high probability, but writing one down explicitly, and saying exactly how good it is, was hard.

Expansion is controlled by eigenvalues. Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix, with eigenvalues $k=\lambda_0\ge\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_{n-1}\ge-k$. The Cheeger-type inequalities (Dodziuk; Alon and Milman; Alon) say

$$\frac{k-\lambda_1}{2}\;\le\;h(X)\;\le\;\sqrt{2k\,(k-\lambda_1)},$$

so a large spectral gap $k-\lambda_1$ is expansion, up to constants. The bottom of the spectrum matters too: a random walk on $X$ forgets its starting point at a rate set by the largest $|\lambda_j|$ other than $\pm k$. So the goal is to push every nontrivial eigenvalue as close to $0$ as possible.

Why $2\sqrt{k-1}$: the tree

Every connected $k$-regular graph is a quotient of the infinite $k$-regular tree $T_k$, its universal cover. Near most vertices a large graph of large girth looks exactly like the tree, so it cannot beat the tree's spectrum, and the tree's adjacency operator has spectrum $[-2\sqrt{k-1},\,2\sqrt{k-1}]$ (Kesten). Counting closed walks makes this precise: for any family of $k$-regular graphs, the largest nontrivial $|\lambda|$ is at least $2\sqrt{k-1}-o(1)$. This is the Alon–Boppana bound, (1.1) below. A Ramanujan graph is one that meets it exactly: it is as good as the tree.

The paper constructs, for every prime $p\equiv1\pmod 4$, an explicit infinite family of $(p+1)$-regular Ramanujan graphs.

1.Introduction

For a $k$-regular graph $X=X_{n,k}$ on $n$ vertices, the paper writes $\lambda(X)$ for the largest $|\lambda_j|$ among the eigenvalues other than $\pm k$. (When $X$ is connected, $k$ is a simple eigenvalue, and $-k$ is an eigenvalue, also simple, exactly when $X$ is bipartite.)

Definition 1.1. A graph $X_{n,k}$ is a Ramanujan graph if $\lambda(X)\le2\sqrt{k-1}$.
$$\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,\lambda(X_{n,k})\;\ge\;2\sqrt{k-1}.\tag{1.1}$$

The bound (1.1) is due to Alon and Boppana; Proposition 4.2 proves it. It says $2\sqrt{k-1}$ is the floor for any infinite family, so Ramanujan graphs are optimal. The paper notes that its graphs are the best explicit expanders known at the time.

The graphs and the count behind them

Let $p\ne q$ be primes, both $\equiv1\pmod4$. The graph $X^{p,q}$ will be a $(p+1)$-regular Cayley graph of $\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ if the Legendre symbol $\leg pq=1$, and of $\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ if $\leg pq=-1$. Its $p+1$ generators come from the ways of writing $p=a_0^2+a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2$ with $a_0>0$ odd and $a_1,a_2,a_3$ even. There are exactly $p+1$ of these, by Jacobi's four-square theorem:

$$r_4(n)=8\sum_{d\mid n,\ 4\nmid d}d.\tag{1.2}$$

The proofs need the same kind of count for a different form. Let

$$Q(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=x_1^2+4q^2x_2^2+4q^2x_3^2+4q^2x_4^2,\tag{1.3}$$

and let $r_Q(n)$ be the number of solutions of

$$Q(v)=n,\qquad v\in\Z^4.\tag{1.4}$$

There is no closed formula for $r_Q(n)$ like Jacobi's. But the Ramanujan conjecture, in the cases proved by Eichler and completed by Igusa, gives an excellent approximation. For $n=p^k$:

(1.5) Eichler, Igusa $$r_Q(p^k)=C(p^k)+O_\varepsilon\!\big(p^{k(1/2+\varepsilon)}\big)\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,\ \text{for every }\varepsilon>0,$$ $$C(p^k)=\begin{cases}c_1\sum_{d\mid p^k}d & \text{if }\leg pq=1,\\[2pt] c_2\sum_{d\mid p^k}d & \text{if }\leg pq=-1\text{ and }k\text{ is even},\\[2pt] 0 & \text{if }\leg pq=-1\text{ and }k\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}$$

The main term $C(p^k)$ grows like $p^k$; the error is roughly its square root. That square-root saving is exactly what the Ramanujan conjecture provides, and it is where the graphs get their name. The constants $c_1,c_2$ are not computed from modular forms: §4 reads them off the graph (both turn out to be $\frac{4}{q(q^2-1)}$; see Figure 6). That the graphs are Ramanujan will follow from (1.5).

What is proved

Write $g(X)$ for the girth (shortest cycle), $i(X)$ for the independence number, $\chi(X)$ for the chromatic number and $\diam(X)$ for the diameter. Besides the Ramanujan property, the paper proves:

The cases are stated with $\leg qp$ here and with $\leg pq$ everywhere else. Since $p\equiv q\equiv1\pmod 4$, quadratic reciprocity makes them equal.

The main results are the Ramanujan property and the girth bounds. The diameter and independence bounds follow from the Ramanujan property, and the chromatic number from the independence number.

The girth is the striking part. Since $n\approx q^3$, Case i gives $g(X)\gtrsim\frac43\log_{p}n$. Erdős and Sachs had shown by counting that graphs with $g>\log_{k-1}n$ exist; Margulis and then Imrich gave explicit ones with $g\ge\frac49\log_{k-1}n$. So these explicit graphs beat even the random ones. The easy upper bound is $g\le2\log_{k-1}n$, and for $k=3$ Weiss had explicit examples with the same $\frac43$ as here. The bounds in Case ii (due to Alon; see Proposition 5.2) give explicit graphs with arbitrarily large girth and arbitrarily large chromatic number at once.

Where the graphs come from

The $(p+1)$-regular tree is the coset space $\PGL_2(\Q_p)/\PGL_2(\Z_p)$. For a suitable arithmetic discrete subgroup $\Gamma$ of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\Q_p)$, the double coset space $\Gamma\backslash\PGL_2(\Q_p)/\PGL_2(\Z_p)$ is a finite $(p+1)$-regular graph when $\Gamma$ is torsion-free, and the theory of automorphic forms shows these graphs are Ramanujan (Ihara; the authors' earlier work). The graphs $X^{p,q}$ are explicit cases: $\Gamma$ is a congruence subgroup of the units of the Hamilton quaternions over $\Z[1/p]$. Because that quaternion algebra has class number one, the quotients are Cayley graphs.

The plan: §2 constructs $X^{p,q}$; §3 realizes it as a quotient of a free group of quaternions and bounds the girth; §4 proves the Ramanujan property and finds the limiting spectral density; §5 bounds the diameter and related quantities.

2.Construction of $X^{p,q}$

By (1.2), $a_0^2+a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2=p$ has $8(p+1)$ integer solutions $\alpha=(a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3)$. Because $p\equiv1\pmod4$, exactly one coordinate of each solution is odd, and exactly $p+1$ solutions have $a_0>0$ odd and $a_1,a_2,a_3$ even. Fix an integer $i$ with $i^2\equiv-1\pmod q$, which exists because $q\equiv1\pmod 4$. To each of the $p+1$ solutions attach the matrix

$$\tilde\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}a_0+ia_1 & a_2+ia_3\\ -a_2+ia_3 & a_0-ia_1\end{pmatrix}\in\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z),\qquad \det\tilde\alpha=p.\tag{2.1}$$

The Cayley graph of a group $G$ with respect to a symmetric set $S$ has vertex set $G$ and joins $x$ to $sx$ for each $s\in S$. Built on $\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ with these $p+1$ matrices, it is $(p+1)$-regular on $q(q^2-1)$ vertices. If $\leg pq=1$, every generator has square determinant, so they all lie in the index-two subgroup $\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ and that Cayley graph is disconnected. So:

  • if $\leg pq=-1$, $X^{p,q}$ is the Cayley graph of $\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z)$. It is bipartite, with sides $\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ and its complement, and has $n=q(q^2-1)$ vertices;
  • if $\leg pq=1$, $X^{p,q}$ is the Cayley graph of $\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ on $n=q(q^2-1)/2$ vertices. It is not bipartite (end of §3).

Connectedness, that these generators reach the whole group, is Proposition 3.3.

3.Quaternions

The free group and its tree

Let $H(\Z)=\{a_0+a_1\bi+a_2\bj+a_3\bk : a_j\in\Z\}$ be the integral quaternions, with $\bi^2=\bj^2=\bk^2=-1$ and $\bi\bj=-\bj\bi=\bk$. Conjugation is $\bar\alpha=a_0-a_1\bi-a_2\bj-a_3\bk$ and the norm is $N(\alpha)=\alpha\bar\alpha=a_0^2+a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2$. The units are $\pm1,\pm\bi,\pm\bj,\pm\bk$.

Each of the $8(p+1)$ quaternions of norm $p$ has exactly one associate $\varepsilon\alpha$ ($\varepsilon$ a unit) with $\varepsilon\alpha\equiv1\pmod 2$ and $a_0>0$. These are the $p+1$ elements of $S$ from §2. They come in conjugate pairs $\{\alpha_1,\bar\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_s,\bar\alpha_s\}$ with $s=\frac{p+1}2$. A reduced word in them is one with no $\alpha_j\bar\alpha_j$ or $\bar\alpha_j\alpha_j$.

Lemma 3.1 (Gerritzen–van der Put). Every $\alpha\in H(\Z)$ with $N(\alpha)=p^k$ can be written uniquely as $$\alpha=\varepsilon\,p^r R_m(\alpha_1,\dots,\bar\alpha_s),$$ where $\varepsilon$ is a unit, $2r+m=k$, and $R_m$ is a reduced word of length $m$.
Proof

Existence. Quaternions of odd norm have a theory of greatest common divisors and factorization (Dickson). So $\alpha=\gamma\beta$ with $N(\beta)=p$, and $\beta=\varepsilon' s$ for some $s\in S$; peel $s$ off and repeat. Cancelling any $s\bar s=p$ that appears leaves $\varepsilon p^rR_m$.

Uniqueness is a count. There are $(p+1)p^{m-1}$ reduced words of length $m\ge1$ and one of length $0$, so the number of expressions $\varepsilon p^rR_m$ with $2r+m=k$ is

$$8\Big(\sum_{0\le r\lt k/2}(p+1)p^{k-2r-1}+\delta(k)\Big)=8\,\frac{p^{k+1}-1}{p-1}=8\sum_{d\mid p^k}d,$$

where $\delta(k)=1$ for $k$ even and $0$ for $k$ odd. By Jacobi (1.2) that is exactly the number of $\alpha$ with $N(\alpha)=p^k$. Every $\alpha$ has at least one expression and there are as many expressions as quaternions, so each $\alpha$ has exactly one.

Uniqueness by counting is the first appearance of the theme of the whole paper: an identity is proved by checking that two finite sets have the same size, and the size of one of them comes from the arithmetic of quadratic forms.

Corollary 3.2. If $\alpha\equiv1\pmod2$ and $N(\alpha)=p^k$, then $\alpha=\pm p^rR_m(\alpha_1,\dots,\bar\alpha_s)$ with $2r+m=k$, and this representation is unique.

Now consider all $\alpha\equiv1\pmod 2$ whose norm is a power of $p$, and identify $\alpha$ with $\beta$ when $\pm p^{v_1}\alpha=p^{v_2}\beta$. The classes form a group $\Lambda(2)$, with $[\alpha][\beta]=[\alpha\beta]$ and $[\alpha]^{-1}=[\bar\alpha]$. By Corollary 3.2 it is free on $[\alpha_1],\dots,[\alpha_s]$. Its Cayley graph with respect to $S$ is therefore the $(p+1)$-regular tree, also called $\Lambda(2)$. This realizes the tree inside number theory.

Why exactly $p+1$, seen from $p$ (not in the paper)

The tree also explains the count. $H$ is ramified only at $2$ and $\infty$, so it splits at $p$: choosing $j$ with $j^2\equiv-1\pmod p$ identifies $H\otimes\F_p$ with $M_2(\F_p)$, and its Severi–Brauer variety, the conic $x^2+y^2+z^2=0$, with $\PP^1(\F_p)$. Each generator has norm $p\equiv0$, so its matrix is rank one and its kernel is a point of $\PP^1(\F_p)$. The $p+1$ generators land on the $p+1$ points, one each, and conjugation swaps kernel and image. Those $p+1$ points are the $p+1$ neighbours of a vertex in the Bruhat–Tits tree of $\PGL_2(\Q_p)$, which is why $\Lambda(2)$ acts simply transitively on it. Figure 1's second view draws this.

Reducing mod $q$

To get finite graphs, divide the tree by a normal subgroup of finite index. For $(m,p)=1$, let $\Lambda(2m)$ be the classes $[\alpha]$ with $2m\mid a_1,a_2,a_3$. It is the kernel of the reduction map $\Lambda(2)\to H(\Z/2m\Z)^*/Z$, where $Z$ is the central subgroup of scalars, so it is normal of finite index. From now on $m=q$. Reduction mod $q$ followed by (2.1) gives a homomorphism

$$\varphi:\Lambda(2)\to\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z),\qquad [\alpha]\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}a_0+ia_1 & a_2+ia_3\\ -a_2+ia_3 & a_0-ia_1\end{pmatrix}.$$
Proposition 3.3. $\operatorname{Image}\varphi=\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ if $\leg pq=-1$, and $\operatorname{Image}\varphi=\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ if $\leg pq=1$.
Proof idea

The generators land in $\PSL_2$ exactly when $p$ is a square mod $q$, and $\PSL_2$ has index two in $\PGL_2$. So it suffices to show the image contains $\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$, that is, to lift every norm-one quaternion $\beta$ mod $q$ to an $\alpha\in H(\Z)$ with $N(\alpha)=p^k$, $\alpha\equiv1\pmod2$ and $\alpha\equiv\beta\pmod q$. Writing $\alpha=a_0+2a_1\bi+2a_2\bj+2a_3\bk$, this asks to represent $p^k$ by $x_1^2+4x_2^2+4x_3^2+4x_4^2$ in a prescribed residue class mod $q$. Malyshev's results on the singular series of Hardy and Littlewood supply such representations once $p^k$ is large and generic. Remark 1 in the paper notes that strong approximation also gives the result.

So $\Lambda(2)/\Lambda(2q)$ is $\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ or $\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$, and the generators go to the matrices (2.1). The graph $X^{p,q}$ is the quotient of the tree $\Lambda(2)$ by $\Lambda(2q)$, and in particular it is connected. The paper calls this realization "more useful for theoretical purposes": every question about $X^{p,q}$ becomes a question about how the tree folds up.

Girth

Theorem 3.4. If $\leg pq=-1$, then $g(X^{p,q})\ge4\log_pq-\log_p4$ and $|X^{p,q}|=q(q^2-1)$. If $\leg pq=1$, then $g(X^{p,q})\ge2\log_pq$ and $|X^{p,q}|=q(q^2-1)/2$.

Since $X^{p,q}$ is a Cayley graph it looks the same from every vertex, so a shortest cycle may be taken through the identity. In the tree picture, that is a nontrivial element $\gamma\in\Lambda(2q)$ of smallest length $t$. Lift it to a quaternion $\tilde\gamma=\beta_1\cdots\beta_t$ with $\beta_j\in S$. Then $N(\tilde\gamma)=p^t$ and $\tilde\gamma=a_0+2qa_1\bi+2qa_2\bj+2qa_3\bk$, with some $a_1,a_2,a_3\ne0$ because $\gamma\ne e$. So

$$p^t=a_0^2+4q^2a_1^2+4q^2a_2^2+4q^2a_3^2.\tag{3.1}$$

If $\leg pq=1$ this already gives $p^t\ge4q^2$, so $t\ge2\log_pq$. If $\leg pq=-1$, reducing (3.1) mod $q$ shows $p^t$ is a square mod $q$, so $t=2r$ is even. Then $a_0$ solves

$$X_0^2\equiv p^t\pmod{q^2},\tag{3.2}$$

and since $(\Z/q^2\Z)^*$ is cyclic, the only solutions are $X_0\equiv\pm p^r\pmod{q^2}$ (3.2)′. If

$$p^t\lt q^4/4,\tag{3.3}$$

then $p^r\lt q^2/2$, so any solution other than $\pm p^r$ has $|X_0|\ge q^2/2$, and (3.1) forces $p^t\gt q^4/4$, a contradiction. Hence $p^t\ge q^4/4$, which is the bound $t\ge4\log_pq-\log_p4$.

The section ends by showing that when $\leg pq=1$, $X^{p,q}$ is not bipartite. A bipartition of the Cayley graph of $G=\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ would make the even-length products of generators an index-two subgroup, and $\PSL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ is simple for $q>3$.

4.Spectrum of $X^{p,q}$

Theorem 4.1. $X^{p,q}$ is a Ramanujan graph.

What every large graph must do

Before the proof, the paper studies the spectrum of a general $X_{n,k}$ as $n\to\infty$, starting with (1.1).

Proposition 4.2. $\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lambda(X_{n,k})\ge2\sqrt{k-1}.$
Proof

The entries of $A^l$ count paths of length $l$: $A^l=(\delta^{(l)}_{ij})$. Taking traces,

$$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\lambda_j^l=\sum_j\delta^{(l)}_{jj}.\tag{4.1}$$

Closed paths in $X$ lift to paths in the universal cover $T_k$, and closed paths in $T_k$ project to closed paths in $X$. So $\delta^{(l)}_{jj}\ge\rho(l)$, the number of closed paths of length $l$ at a vertex of $T_k$, and

$$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\lambda_j^l\ge n\,\rho(l).\tag{4.2}$$

Remove the eigenvalues $\pm k$ (at most two) and bound the rest by $\lambda(X)$:

$$\lambda(X)^{2l}\ge\rho(2l)-\frac{2k^{2l}}{n-2}.\tag{4.3}$$

Finally $\rho(2l)$ is at least the number of first returns, $\frac1l\binom{2l-2}{l-1}k(k-1)^{l-1}$, and $\binom{2l-2}{l-1}^{1/2l}\to2$. Let $n\to\infty$, then $l\to\infty$.

If the girth also tends to infinity, the whole spectrum converges. Put mass $\frac1n$ at each eigenvalue to get a probability measure $\mu_{X_{n,k}}$ on $[-k,k]$.

Proposition 4.3. As $n\to\infty$ with $g(X_{n,k})\to\infty$, the measures $\mu_{X_{n,k}}$ converge weak-$*$ to $\mu_k$, where $$d\mu_k(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sqrt{k-1-t^2/4}}{\pi k\,\big(1-(t/k)^2\big)}\,dt & \text{if }|t|\le2\sqrt{k-1},\\[6pt] 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

It suffices to check moments:

$$\int t^l\,d\mu_{X_{n,k}}(t)\;\longrightarrow\;\int t^l\,d\mu_k(t)\quad\text{for each }l\ge0.\tag{4.4}$$

Once the girth exceeds $l$, every vertex sees a tree out to distance $l$, so $\frac1n\sum_j\lambda_j^l=\rho(l)$ exactly. And $\rho(l)$ is the $l$-th moment of $\mu_k$ (Kesten). Since Theorem 3.4 makes $g(X^{p,q})\to\infty$ as $q\to\infty$, the eigenvalues of $X^{p,q}$, apart from $\pm(p+1)$, fill out $[-2\sqrt p,2\sqrt p]$ with density $\mu_{p+1}$.

Harmonic analysis on the tree

Let $\Gamma$ act freely on $T=T_k$ by isometries with $n=|T/\Gamma|$ finite, and let $L^2(T/\Gamma)$ be the $\Gamma$-periodic functions on $T$. The adjacency operator preserves this space, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis:

$$\Delta u_j=\lambda_ju_j,\qquad u_0(x)=\frac1{\sqrt n},\quad\lambda_0=p+1=k.\tag{4.5}$$

The paper writes $\Delta$ for the adjacency operator, not the Laplacian.

Build $\Gamma$-periodic kernels from distances. For an integer $l\ge0$, the point-pair function and its periodization are

$$k_l(x,y)=\begin{cases}1 & d(x,y)=l,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\tag{4.6}$$
$$K_l(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}k_l(\gamma x,y).\tag{4.7}$$

$K_l(x,y)$ counts the points of the orbit $\Gamma x$ at distance exactly $l$ from $y$. Because $\Delta$ commutes with the operator $k_l$, the expansion of $K_l$ in the eigenbasis is diagonal:

$$K_l(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}h_j(l)\,u_j(x)\,u_j(y).\tag{4.8}$$

Summing alternate shells, out to distance $t$,

$$L_t(x,y)=\sum_{0\le r\le t/2}K_{t-2r}(x,y),\tag{4.9}$$

gives coefficients with a clean closed form (computed in the authors' earlier work):

$$L_t(x,y)=p^{t/2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\sin(t+1)\theta_j}{\sin\theta_j}\,u_j(x)\,u_j(y),\tag{4.10}$$
$$\lambda_j=2\sqrt p\cos\theta_j.\tag{4.11}$$

The angle $\theta_j$ is real exactly when $|\lambda_j|\le2\sqrt p$. Otherwise it is complex: $\theta_j=i\mu$ with $\mu>0$ if $\lambda_j>2\sqrt p$, and $\theta_j=\pi+i\mu$ if $\lambda_j\lt-2\sqrt p$. For instance $\theta_0=i\log\sqrt p$, and $\theta_{n-1}=\pi+i\log\sqrt p$ when $\lambda_{n-1}=-(p+1)$. So the Ramanujan property is the statement that every nontrivial $\theta_j$ is real.

Now take $T=\Lambda(2)$ and $\Gamma=\Lambda(2q)$. On the diagonal, $K_l(x,x)$ counts $\gamma\in\Lambda(2q)$ with $d(\gamma x,x)=l$, and normality of $\Lambda(2q)$ moves the count to the identity:

$$K_l(x,x)=\big|\{\gamma\in\Lambda(2q):d(\gamma e,e)=l\}\big|,\tag{4.12}$$
$$L_t(x,x)=L_t(e,e)\quad\text{for all }x\in\Lambda(2),\ t\ge0.\tag{4.13}$$

Counting quaternions is taking a trace

Here the two halves of the paper meet. Return to the form $Q=x_1^2+(2q)^2x_2^2+(2q)^2x_3^2+(2q)^2x_4^2$ of (1.3). A solution of $Q(v)=p^k$ is a quaternion $\alpha$ of norm $p^k$ with $2q\mid a_1,a_2,a_3$. By Corollary 3.2 each one is $\pm p^rR_t$ with $2r+t=k$ and $[\alpha]\in\Lambda(2q)$, uniquely. So the solutions are counted by elements of $\Lambda(2q)$ at distance $k,k-2,k-4,\dots$ from $e$, twice over for the sign:

$$r_Q(p^k)=2\sum_{0\le r\le k/2}\big|\{\alpha\in\Lambda(2q):d(\alpha,e)=k-2r\}\big|,$$

that is,

$$r_Q(p^k)=2L_k(e,e)=2L_k(x,x).\tag{4.14}$$

The sum is printed over $r\lt k/2$. It must include $r=k/2$ (the identity, $\alpha=\pm p^{k/2}$), as in (4.9); without it $r_Q(p^2)=2$ would be counted as $0$.

Average (4.14) over the $n$ vertices of $T/\Lambda(2q)$ and insert (4.10). Since the $u_j$ are orthonormal, only the diagonal survives:

(4.15) $$r_Q(p^k)=\frac{2p^{k/2}}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\sin(k+1)\theta_j}{\sin\theta_j}.$$

The left side is arithmetic: it counts integer solutions of a quadratic equation. The right side is spectral: it sums a function of the eigenvalues of $X^{p,q}$. The paper notes that the spectrum of the adjacency operator on $\Lambda(2q)$-periodic functions is the spectrum of the quotient graph, and calls (4.15) the key relation. Figure 5 checks it, exactly, on real graphs.

The theta series and the main term

Combine (4.15) with the Eichler–Igusa estimate (1.5):

$$C(p^k)+O_\varepsilon\big(p^{k(1/2+\varepsilon)}\big)=\frac{2p^{k/2}}n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\sin(k+1)\theta_j}{\sin\theta_j}\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0.\tag{4.16}$$

Where $C$ comes from: $r_Q(n)$ is the $n$-th coefficient of the theta function $\theta(z)=\sum_{v\in\Z^4}e^{2\pi iQ(v)z}$, a modular form of weight two (Hecke). It splits as a combination of Eisenstein series plus a cusp form. The cusp form contributes the error term, by the Ramanujan bound; the Eisenstein series contribute the "singular series" $C(n)$. From the known Fourier expansions of Eisenstein series, $C$ has the shape

$$C(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,F(d),\qquad F:\NN\to\C\ \text{periodic of period }4q^2.$$

The proof never computes $F$. Instead it compares $C(p^k)$ with the trivial eigenvalues, using a lemma about divisor sums with periodic weights.

Lemma 4.4. Let $G:\NN\to\C$ be periodic, and suppose $\sum_{d\mid p^k}d\,G(d)=o(p^k)$ as $k\to\infty$. Then $\sum_{d\mid p^k}d\,G(d)=0$ for all $k$.
Proof

Let $\alpha_k=\sum_{d\mid p^k}dG(d)$. Then

$$\frac{\alpha_k}{p^k}-\frac{\alpha_{k-1}}{p^{k-1}p}=G(p^k).\tag{4.17}$$

The left side tends to $0$. But $k\mapsto G(p^k)$ is periodic, so it must vanish identically.

Lemma 4.4 and Theorem 4.4 share a number in the paper; both keep it here.

Rewriting (4.16) with the shape of $C$:

$$\sum_{d\mid p^k}d\,F(d)+O_\varepsilon\big(p^{k(1/2+\varepsilon)}\big)=\frac{2p^{k/2}}n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\sin(k+1)\theta_j}{\sin\theta_j}\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0.\tag{4.18}$$

The two cases

Case i, $\leg pq=-1$. Here $X^{p,q}$ is bipartite, so its eigenvalues come in pairs $\pm\lambda$, and $\pm(p+1)$ are simple: $|\lambda_j|\lt p+1$ for $j\ne0,n-1$. The trivial pair contributes $0$ to the right side of (4.18) when $k$ is odd, and $\frac{4(p^{k+1}-1)}{n(p-1)}=\frac4n\sum_{d\mid p^k}d$ when $k$ is even. Every other term is $o(p^k)$. So the difference of the two divisor sums is $o(p^k)$, and Lemma 4.4 makes it exactly zero:

$$C(p^k)=\begin{cases}0 & k\text{ odd},\\[2pt] \dfrac{4(p^{k+1}-1)}{n(p-1)} & k\text{ even}.\end{cases}\tag{4.19}$$

Cancelling these identical leading terms leaves, for even $k\to\infty$,

$$\sum_{j\ne0,\,n-1}\frac{\sin(k+1)\theta_j}{\sin\theta_j}=O_\varepsilon\big(p^{\varepsilon k}\big)\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0,$$

and this forces every remaining $\theta_j$ to be real, so $|\lambda_j|\le2\sqrt p$.

The range of this last sum is printed once as $j=1$ and once as $j=2$ to $n-2$. It is every $j$ except $0$ and $n-1$, the eigenvalues $\pm(p+1)$.

Why "this forces every $\theta_j$ to be real"

The paper says "clearly". If $\theta_j=i\mu$ with $\mu>0$, the term is $\frac{\sinh(k+1)\mu}{\sinh\mu}$, which is positive and grows like $e^{k\mu}$. If $\theta_j=\pi+i\mu$, it is $(-1)^k\frac{\sinh(k+1)\mu}{\sinh\mu}$, which is also positive when $k$ is even. A real $\theta_j$ contributes at most $k+1$ in absolute value. So for even $k$ the non-real terms all have the same sign and cannot cancel, and a single one of them would outgrow $p^{\varepsilon k}$ for small $\varepsilon$. That is why the argument runs over even $k$.

Case ii, $\leg pq=1$. Now $X^{p,q}$ is not bipartite (end of §3), so $|\lambda_j|\lt p+1$ for all $j\ne0$, and (4.16) reads $C(p^k)+O_\varepsilon(p^{k(1/2+\varepsilon)})=\frac{2(p^{k+1}-1)}{n(p-1)}+o(p^k)$. By Lemma 4.4,

$$C(p^k)=\frac{2(p^{k+1}-1)}{n(p-1)},\tag{4.20}$$

and this time $\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\sin(k+1)\theta_j}{\sin\theta_j}=O_\varepsilon(p^{k\varepsilon})$, so every $\theta_j$ with $j\ge1$ is real. That proves Theorem 4.1. ∎

In both cases the constant is the same, $c_1=c_2=\frac4{q(q^2-1)}$, since $n$ doubles from Case ii to Case i. The proof learns the Eisenstein part of a theta series from the trivial eigenvalues of a graph.

Remark 2: the referee's shortcut

A referee pointed out that (4.15) needs no harmonic analysis on the tree. Define polynomials by

$$H_0(x)=1,\quad H_1(x)=x,\quad H_t(x)=xH_{t-1}(x)-pH_{t-2}(x)\ \ (t\ge2),$$

and the operator $L_t=H_t(\Delta)$. On a $(p+1)$-regular graph, $H_t(\Delta)$ counts non-backtracking walks of length $t,t-2,t-4,\dots$, so the counting argument behind (4.14) gives $\TR(L_t)=n\,r_Q(p^t)/2$. On the other hand $\TR(L_t)=\sum_jH_t(\lambda_j)$, and with $\lambda_j=2\sqrt p\cos\theta_j$ this is $\sum_jp^{t/2}\frac{\sin(t+1)\theta_j}{\sin\theta_j}$. Equating the two traces is (4.15). Figure 5 computes exactly this.

The graphs $Y^{p,q}$

The same generators give a smaller family. $\PGL_2(\Z/q\Z)$ acts on the projective line $\PP^1(\F_q)=\{0,1,\dots,q-1,\infty\}$ by fractional linear maps. Join $\xi$ to $\gamma\xi$ for each $\gamma\in S$: the result is a $(p+1)$-regular graph $Y^{p,q}$ on $q+1$ vertices. An eigenfunction $f$ of $Y^{p,q}$ pulls back to $F(g)=f(g(0))$ on $X^{p,q}$ with the same eigenvalue, so $\spec Y^{p,q}\subseteq\spec X^{p,q}$ and $Y^{p,q}$ is Ramanujan. It remains to rule out $-(p+1)$. That could only happen when $\leg pq=-1$, with $F=1$ on $\PSL_2$ and $-1$ on its complement. But $F$ is constant on the stabilizer of $0$, the lower-triangular matrices, which contains elements of both. So:

Theorem 4.4. The graphs $Y^{p,q}$ are non-bipartite Ramanujan graphs of order $q+1$ and degree $p+1$.

5.Diameter and other quantities

Since $X^{p,q}\cong\Lambda(2)/\Lambda(2q)=T/\Lambda(2q)$, the diameter is a statement about orbits in the tree:

$$\diam(X^{p,q})=\sup_{x,y\in T}\ \inf_{\gamma\in\Lambda(2q)}d(x,\gamma y).\tag{5.1}$$

If two vertices are farther apart than $l$ in $X^{p,q}$, then no orbit point is within $l$, so $K_m(x,y)=0$ for every $m\le l$, and

$$\sum_{m\le l}a_mK_m(x,y)=0\tag{5.2}$$

for any coefficients. Choose them from the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind:

$$\cos(l\arccos x)=\sum_{m=0}^la_mx^m.\tag{5.3}$$

The paper calls this polynomial $H_l$, the same name as the different polynomials of Remark 2. It also refers to (4.4) and (4.5) for $k_m$ and $K_m$, which are (4.6) and (4.7).

As in §4,

$$\sum_{m\le l}a_mK_m(x,y)=p^{l/2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\cos(l\theta_j)\,u_j(x)\,u_j(y).\tag{5.4}$$

For even $l$, the trivial eigenvalue contributes $\frac{p^l+1}{2n}$. By Theorem 4.1 every other $\cos(l\theta_j)$ has absolute value at most $1$, and $\sum_j|u_j(x)|^2=1$. So if $x,y$ are farther apart than $l$, then $\frac{p^l+1}{2n}\le p^{l/2}$, which forces $p^{l/2}\le2n$, that is $l\le2\log_p(2n)$.

Theorem 5.1. If $n=|X^{p,q}|$, then $\diam(X^{p,q})\le2\log_pn+2\log_p2+1$.

In the other direction the paper notes $\diam(X^{p,q})\ge\log_pn$, up to a bounded term: a ball of radius $r$ in a $(p+1)$-regular graph has at most $1+(p+1)\frac{p^r-1}{p-1}$ vertices. So the diameter is pinned down within a factor of two. For the rest of the section, $\leg pq=1$.

Proposition 5.2 (Alon). If $X_{n,k}$ is a non-bipartite Ramanujan graph, then $i(X)\le\dfrac{2\sqrt{k-1}}{k}\,n$.
Proof

Let $A$ be an independent set with $|A|=r$. Define

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}1 & x\in A,\\ -c & x\notin A,\end{cases}\qquad r-(n-r)c=0,\tag{5.5}$$

so that $f\perp1$. The Ramanujan property, for a non-bipartite graph, gives

$$\|\Delta f\|_2^2\le4(k-1)\|f\|_2^2.\tag{5.6}$$

On the other hand every neighbour of a vertex of $A$ lies outside $A$, so $\Delta f=-ck$ on $A$ and

$$\|\Delta f\|_2^2\ge c^2k^2r.\tag{5.7}$$

Together,

$$c^2k^2r\le4(k-1)\big(r+c^2(n-r)\big).\tag{5.8}$$

With $c=\nu/(1-\nu)$ and $\nu=r/n$, this simplifies to $\nu^2k^2\le4(k-1)(1-\nu)\le4(k-1)$, so $\nu\le\frac{2\sqrt{k-1}}k$.

Each colour class of a proper colouring is independent, so an upper bound on $i(X)$ is a lower bound on $\chi(X)$:

$$\chi(X_{n,k})\ge\frac{k}{2\sqrt{k-1}}\quad\text{for non-bipartite Ramanujan }X_{n,k}.\tag{5.9}$$

Remark 3 notes that (5.9) also follows directly from Hoffman's eigenvalue bound. The paper closes by observing that the explicit graphs $X^{p,q}$ share many extremal properties of random graphs. Remark 4 records that Margulis independently obtained similar results.

Afterwards

  • 1988. Margulis gives an independent construction of the same kind of graphs.
  • 1992, 1994. Chiu builds cubic Ramanujan graphs ($p=2$). Morgenstern builds $(q+1)$-regular Ramanujan graphs for every prime power $q$, replacing $\Q$ by function fields over $\F_q$, where Drinfeld's work supplies the Ramanujan bound.
  • 2003–2008. Friedman proves Alon's conjecture: a random $k$-regular graph has $\lambda(X)\le2\sqrt{k-1}+\varepsilon$ with high probability. Random graphs are almost Ramanujan.
  • 2005. Lubotzky, Samuels and Vishne, and independently Li, construct Ramanujan complexes, higher-dimensional analogues built on buildings instead of trees.
  • 2015. Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava prove that bipartite Ramanujan graphs exist in every degree, by interlacing families of polynomials, with no number theory.
  • 2024. Huang, McKenzie and Yau show that a positive proportion of random regular graphs, about 69%, are exactly Ramanujan.

Still open: whether infinite families of non-bipartite $k$-regular Ramanujan graphs exist for every $k$, and explicit constructions in degrees that are not one more than a prime power.
