Cannon's Conjecture, by Modulus

How a hyperbolic group whose boundary is a 2-sphere is shown to act geometrically on hyperbolic 3-space, following the September 2026 proof section by section.

Status (October 7, 2026)

The paper A Modulus Proof of Cannon's Conjecture (OpenAI, dated September 23, 2026) was posted on October 6, 2026 in the openai/math repository (commit adc7f12). It has not been refereed and has not yet been independently checked.

The companion Lean file CannonGeometricAction.lean formalizes the statement: thin triangles in a Cayley graph, the ray boundary homeomorphic to $S^2$, a proper cocompact action on $\Hyp^3$ with finite kernel. Its proof is sorry. It is a comparator challenge, not a machine-checked proof.

This page explains the argument as the paper gives it. Section and lemma numbers match the paper. The figures are models: they show how each step works on concrete examples, and they are not part of the proof.

1The conjecture

A finitely generated group $G$ is hyperbolic if geodesic triangles in its Cayley graph are uniformly thin. Its Gromov boundary $\partial G$ is the set of geodesic rays from the identity, where two rays are identified if they stay a bounded distance apart. Two rays are close in $\partial G$ if they fellow-travel for a long time. The model example is a group acting geometrically (properly and cocompactly by isometries) on $\Hyp^3$. Its Cayley graph is quasi-isometric to $\Hyp^3$, so its boundary is the sphere at infinity $\partial\Hyp^3 = S^2$. Cannon's conjecture is the converse: the topology at infinity is enough to recover the geometry.

Theorem 1.1 (Cannon's conjecture). Let $G$ be a hyperbolic group with $\partial G$ homeomorphic to $S^2$. There is a homomorphism $\rho\colon G\to\Isom(\Hyp^3)$ for which the action on $\Hyp^3$ is proper and cocompact. Its kernel is finite.

The kernel may be nontrivial: finite normal subgroups act trivially on the boundary. The image may contain orientation-reversing isometries. The paper keeps both cases rather than assuming them away. For torsion-free $G$, the quotient $\Hyp^3/G$ is a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold (Section 8).

Earlier work. The paper builds on three lines of work.

  • Discrete conformal geometry. Cannon's combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem (1994), Cannon–Swenson (1998), and Cannon–Floyd–Parry turned the problem into estimates on combinatorial moduli of curve families at small scales.
  • Analysis on metric spheres. Bonk and Kleiner gave a metric uniformization criterion (2002). They proved the conjecture when the Ahlfors-regular conformal dimension of $\partial G$ is attained (2005). Bourdon–Kleiner turned the criterion into a bound on combinatorial 2-modulus (2013). That bound is the criterion this proof uses.
  • Algebraic and topological routes. Marković's criterion uses quasiconvex surface subgroups (2013). Haïssinsky treated cubulated groups (2015). Groves–Haïssinsky–Manning–Osajda–Sisto–Walsh developed group-theoretic drilling (2024–26).

The new paper takes the analytic route all the way. It proves the Bourdon–Kleiner modulus bound directly, using only hyperbolicity and the fact that the boundary is a topological sphere.

2The sphere at infinity

Fix a Cayley graph $\Gamma$ with identity vertex $e$, and write $|v| = d_\Gamma(e,v)$. The Gromov product $(y|z)_e = \tfrac12\big(d(e,y)+d(e,z)-d(y,z)\big)$ measures how long the geodesics from $e$ to $y$ and $z$ travel together. A visual metric on $Z=\partial G$ converts this time into distance:

$$C_v^{-1}\,a^{-(y|z)_e}\ \le\ d(y,z)\ \le\ C_v\,a^{-(y|z)_e}\qquad (a>1). \tag{2.1}$$

Write $r_k = a^{-k}$. A ball of radius $r_k$ about $x$ consists, up to bounded error, of the endpoints of rays that pass near the depth-$k$ vertex of a ray to $x$.

2.1Expansions

Self-similarity of $\partial G$ comes from left multiplication. If $v$ is the depth-$k$ vertex on a ray to $x$, then $h=v^{-1}$ moves $v$ back to the identity. On the boundary it magnifies the ball $B(x,r_k)$ to unit size. The paper makes a point of also tracking what $h$ does to the complement of that ball.

Lemma 2.1 (Expansion formula). Let $v$ be the depth-$k$ vertex on a based ray to $x$, put $h=v^{-1}$, $R=r_k$ and $D_y=\max\{R,d(x,y)\}$. Then, with constants independent of all choices, $$c_E\,\frac{R\,d(y,z)}{D_yD_z}\ \le\ d(hy,hz)\ \le\ C_E\,\frac{R\,d(y,z)}{D_yD_z}. \tag{2.2}$$ If $d(x,z_0)\ge\varepsilon_*$ and $p = hz_0$, then $d(hy,p)\lesssim R/D_y$ for all $y$, with $\asymp$ when $d(x,y)\le\varepsilon_*/2$. Also $(v^{-1}|p)_e\ge k-C(\varepsilon_*)$.

On $B(x,CR)$ the map $h$ is a similarity with factor $R^{-1}$, up to bounded distortion. Outside that ball it behaves like an inversion. Everything at definite distance from $x$ lands within $O(R)$ of one point, the puncture $p=hz_0$. This is how a loxodromic Möbius transformation acts on the Riemann sphere, and Figure 1 shows the picture for a real Kleinian group. Punctures play a central role in §4 and §6. Two expansions with different punctures pull a function's level sets toward different points, and that difference drives the contradictions.

Two more facts complete the toolkit. The action is uniformly quasi-Möbius: every $g\in G$ distorts cross-ratios $\frac{d(x,z)d(y,w)}{d(x,w)d(y,z)}$ by a bounded factor, independent of $g$. And Lemma 2.2 (“prefixes”) lets every vertex be treated as a point on a ray, so $h = v^{-1}$ can be used for an arbitrary $v$.

2.2Packing and paths

Lemma 2.3 (Packing). There are $Q\ge2$ and $C_P$ such that an $s$-separated subset of a ball of radius $t$ has at most $C_P(t/s)^Q$ points.
Lemma 2.4 (Controlled paths). There is $L\ge1$ such that any two points of $B(x,s)$ are joined by a path in $B(x,Ls)$. Any two points of a continuum can be joined by a path in any prescribed neighborhood of it.

Lemma 2.4 is the first place the hypothesis $\partial G\cong S^2$ enters. To join two points of a small ball, expand the ball to unit size. There the two points are a definite distance from the puncture, so they can be joined on the sphere by a path that avoids a neighborhood of the puncture. Pulling that path back by $h^{-1}$ keeps it inside $B(x,Ls)$.

2.3The classical reduction: combinatorial modulus

At scale $r_n$, choose a maximal $r_n$-separated set $T_n\subset Z$ and use the cells $B(q,2r_n)$, $q\in T_n$. For a family $\mathcal F$ of paths, the combinatorial 2-modulus is the least total squared weight that charges every path at least $1$:

$$\Mod_2(\mathcal F,T_n)=\min_{\omega\ge0}\Big\{\sum_{q\in T_n}\omega(q)^2\ :\ \sum_{B(q,2r_n)\cap\pi\neq\emptyset}\omega(q)\ \ge 1\ \text{ for every }\pi\in\mathcal F\Big\}.$$

Fix a small $d_0>0$ and let $\mathcal F_0$ be the paths of diameter at least $d_0$. Define

$$M(n)=\Mod_2(\mathcal F_0,T_n). \tag{2.5}$$

$M(n)$ measures how expensive it is to block all macroscopic paths with cells of size $r_n$. On the round sphere it stays bounded as the mesh refines, because 2-dimensional conformal energy is scale invariant. If $M(n)\to\infty$, the space carries more macroscopic paths than a conformally 2-dimensional sphere can. Figure 2 computes these moduli on a planar hexagonal grid.

Theorem 2.5 (Bonk–Kleiner; Bourdon–Kleiner). If $\sup_n M(n)\lt \infty$, then $(Z,d)$ is quasi-Möbius homeomorphic to the round sphere.

This is Corollary 3.5 of Bourdon–Kleiner, which they derive from Bonk–Kleiner's Theorem 10.4. Its hypotheses are that $Z$ is an arcwise connected, approximately self-similar metric sphere, and that the moduli of a fixed family of graph approximations are bounded. Lemma 2.1 gives self-similarity: every ball, rescaled by its radius, is uniformly bi-Lipschitz to an open subset of $Z$. The cells above are $\kappa$-approximations for $\kappa>2a$. No Coxeter-group assumption is needed. So the entire problem reduces to one inequality, $\sup_n M(n)\lt \infty$.

2.4From the boundary to hyperbolic space

Proposition 2.6. If $(Z,d)$ is quasi-Möbius homeomorphic to the round sphere, then $G$ admits a proper cocompact isometric action on $\Hyp^3$ with finite kernel.

This step is classical, but the paper writes it out so that orientation-reversing elements are handled.

  1. Uniformly quasiconformal. Conjugate by the uniformization $\psi\colon Z\to S^2$. The cross-ratio bound gives one quasi-Möbius distortion function for the whole group. Let two points approach $x$ at equal distance while a fourth stays away. In the limit, the infinitesimal linear distortion of each $f$ is bounded independently of $f$ and $x$. So the group is uniformly quasiconformal. A reversing element is treated after composing with a conformal reflection.
  2. Invariant conformal structure (Sullivan, Tukia). At almost every point $x$, the conformal structures on $T_xS^2$ are positive definite forms modulo scaling. They form a hyperbolic plane $\mathcal C_x$, and pullback by any invertible linear map, of either orientation, is an isometry of it. The orbit $\{f^*q_{\rm round}(x)\}$ is bounded by the uniform quasiconformal bound, so it has a unique hyperbolic circumcenter $q(x)$. The chain rule permutes the orbit, so $f^*q=q$ for every $f\in G$. Measurability follows by taking circumcenters of finite subsets of an enumeration.
  3. Straighten. The measurable Riemann mapping theorem gives a quasiconformal $F$ with $F^*q_{\rm round}=q$. Each $FfF^{-1}$ is then conformal or anticonformal, so it is a Möbius or anti-Möbius map, and it extends uniquely to an isometry of $\Hyp^3$.
  4. Proper and cocompact. $G$ acts properly and cocompactly on the space $T(Z)$ of ordered distinct triples. This follows from (2.2): compact sets of triples are those with pairwise distances bounded below, and expansions spread any triple out. The map $T(S^2)\cong I/H\to I/K_o\cong\Hyp^3$, with $I=\Isom(\Hyp^3)$ and $H$ of order two, is continuous, onto, proper and equivariant. So properness and cocompactness pass to $\Hyp^3$. The kernel fixes a point, so it is finite.

3A scalar limit from unbounded modulus

From now on, assume $M(n)$ is unbounded. This section produces a continuous, nonconstant function $u$. Two properties of $u$ will turn out to be incompatible:

  • $u$ has a finite energy measure $\mu$. Its oscillation on small balls is controlled by $\mu$ (3.6).
  • $u$ is a limit of functions $H_N$ whose finite-cell energy at mesh $r_N$ tends to zero. This stays true after composing with any fixed group element (Lemma 3.5).

In the Euclidean plane, a function that climbs from $0$ to $1$ across a square has Dirichlet energy at least the modulus of the square. Vanishing energy is impossible there. It is exactly what unbounded modulus provides.

3.1Record scales and crossing duality

Let $\lambda$ be a growth rate for $M$. If $\limsup M(n)^{1/n}>1$, pick $1\lt \lambda\lt \limsup M(n)^{1/n}$; otherwise $\lambda=1$. Either way $M(n)/\lambda^n$ is unbounded. A record index $N$ is one where $M(n)/\lambda^n$ reaches a new maximum. Along records $M(N)\to\infty$, and coarser scales are controlled by finer ones:

$$M(N-l)\ \le\ \lambda^{-l}\,M(N)\qquad(0\le l\le N). \tag{3.1}$$

When $\lambda>1$, coarser scales are exponentially cheaper. This dichotomy decides between §4 and §§5–6.

Lemma 3.1 (Localization to a rectangle). There are coordinate rectangles $Q=[0,1]^2\subset\widehat Q=[0,1]\times[-1,2]\subset Z$ and a subsequence of records along which the left-to-right crossing modulus of $Q$ is at least $c\,M(N)$.

Every macroscopic path contains a crossing of one of finitely many coordinate strips. One strip therefore carries a fixed fraction of the modulus. Coordinates are used only for topology and interpolation. All distances are still measured with $d$.

Lemma 3.2 (Crossings and blockers). In a closed coordinate rectangle, (i) every continuum meeting both lateral edges meets every top-to-bottom path; (ii) every compact set meeting all top-to-bottom paths contains a continuum meeting both lateral edges.

Part (i) is the Jordan curve theorem. Part (ii) is the content of the Hex theorem, and its proof is the labeling argument in Figure 4. Cover the rectangle by a fine square grid and mark the squares meeting the blocker $K$. Label A the unmarked squares reachable from the top through edge-adjacent unmarked squares, and add a row of A squares above the grid. Label every other square B, and add a row of B squares below. The interfaces between A and B form a graph whose interior vertices have even degree. Down each lateral side the labels start at A and end at B, so each side has an odd number of interface ends. Some interface component must therefore join the two sides. The B squares along it are marked and meet at least at corners, so they form a lateral crossing inside $K$. A Hausdorff limit as the mesh shrinks gives the continuum.

Lemma 3.3 (Crossing dual weights). For each retained $N$ there are nonnegative weights $\rho_q$, $q\in T_N$, admissible for top-to-bottom crossings of $Q$, with $$\sum_{q\in T_N}\rho_q^2\ \le\ \frac{C}{M(N)}. \tag{3.2}$$
Proof

Let $P$ minimize the Euclidean norm on the convex hull of the finitely many left-to-right incidence vectors $v$. Minimality gives $\langle P,v\rangle\ge\|P\|^2$ for every such $v$, so $P/\|P\|^2$ is admissible. Conversely, every admissible $\omega$ satisfies $\langle\omega,P\rangle\ge1$, so $\|\omega\|\ge\|P\|^{-1}$. Hence $\Mod_{LR}=\|P\|^{-2}$. By Lemma 3.2(i), every top-to-bottom incidence vector has inner product at least $1$ with every $v$, and hence with their convex combination $P$. So $\rho=P$ is admissible for top-to-bottom paths, with $\sum\rho^2=\|P\|^2=1/\Mod_{LR}\le 1/(cM(N))$. This is the probabilistic modulus formula of Albin–Poggi-Corradini, and Figure 2 computes it.

3.2Continuous functions with separating levels

Turn the dual weights into a function. For $x\in Q$, let $b_N(x)$ be the cheapest $\rho$-cost of a path from the top edge to $x$, where a path's cost $\ell_\rho(\pi)$ is the sum of $\rho_q$ over the cells it meets. Clip at $1$, and extend by $0$ on the top band and $1$ on the bottom band. Admissibility forces the value $1$ on the bottom edge. Along any path, the raw values change by at most the path's cost. Interpolate on a fine triangulation to get a continuous $U^0_N$.

Continuity alone does not give level sets that every straddling path must meet. The paper therefore takes two path envelopes:

$$F_N(x)=\inf_{\pi:\,x\to\text{top}}\ \max_\pi U^0_N,\qquad H_N(x)=\sup_{\pi:\,x\to\text{bottom}}\ \inf_\pi F_N .$$
Lemma 3.4 (Regularization and level continua). $F_N,H_N$ are continuous with the band values, and $\osc_K H_N\le\osc_K F_N\le\osc_K U^0_N$ for every path $K$. For each $0\lt t\lt 1$, the level $\{H_N=t\}\cap Q$ contains a lateral crossing continuum $C_{N,t}$ of diameter at least a fixed $d_1>0$. Every path whose endpoint values strictly straddle $t$ meets $C_{N,t}$.

The envelopes ensure that every point of $\{H_N\lt t\}$ is joined to the top band inside that set, and every point of $\{H_N>t\}$ to the bottom band. Take a path that straddles $t$ and extend both ends through the strict sides to a full top-to-bottom crossing. The extensions avoid the level set. The full crossing meets the lateral continuum, so the original path does too.

3.3Oscillation and the annular estimate

For a function $f$, compact $K$ and $J\ge2$, define the finite-cell energy

$$E^{(J)}_n(f;K)=\sum_{q\in T_n,\ B(q,2r_n)\cap K\ne\emptyset}\big(\osc_{B(q,Jr_n)}f\big)^2.$$
Lemma 3.5 (Local oscillation). $\osc_{B(x,Jr_N)}H_N\le\sum_{d(q,x)\le C(J)r_N}\rho_q$. Consequently, for every fixed $g\in G$ and compact $K'\subset gU$, where $U$ is the interior of $\widehat Q$, $$E^{(J)}_N(H_N\circ g^{-1};K')\ \le\ \frac{C(g,K',J)}{M(N)}\ \longrightarrow\ 0.$$

The next estimate is where the group's self-similarity enters. It compares the oscillation of $H_N$ across a ball of radius $R=r_l$ with the dual energy in the surrounding annulus. The price is a modulus at the coarser relative scale $N-l$.

Lemma 3.6 (Annular estimate). For compact $K\subset U$ there are $R_*$ and $m_0$ such that if $x\in K$, $R=r_l\le R_*$, $N-l\ge m_0$, then any two points joined by a path in $B(x,R/2)$ have $H_N$-values differing by $\Delta$ with $$\Delta^2\ \le\ C\,M(N-l)\sum_{q\in T_N,\ R/4\le d(q,x)\le4R}\rho_q^2. \tag{3.5}$$
Proof idea

Take any value $t$ between the two endpoint values. The level continuum $C_{N,t}$ meets the short path, and its diameter exceeds $4R$, so it leaves $B(x,2R)$ and crosses the annulus $R\le d(\cdot,x)\le 2R$. Expand at $(x,l)$. The annulus becomes macroscopic and the mesh $r_N$ becomes $r_{N-l}$. Macroscopic admissible weights $\omega$ at that scale, with $\sum\omega^2=M(N-l)$, pull back to weights $\sigma_q$ on annulus cells, with $\sum\sigma_q^2\le CM(N-l)$. Every crossing is charged at least $1$, and each charged cell has $t$ in its range interval $I_q$, so $1\le\sum_q\sigma_q\mathbf 1_{I_q}(t)$. Integrate over $t$ and apply Cauchy–Schwarz: $\Delta\le CM(N-l)^{1/2}(\sum|I_q|^2)^{1/2}$. Lemma 3.5 then bounds $|I_q|$ by nearby $\rho$'s.

3.4The limiting function and measure

Normalize the dual energies into measures $\mu_N=M(N)\sum_q\rho_q^2\,\delta_q$. Their total mass is bounded by (3.2). Combining (3.5) with the record inequality (3.1) gives

$$|H_N(x)-H_N(y)|^2\ \le\ C\,\lambda^{-l}\,\mu_N\{r_l/4\le d(x,\cdot)\le 4r_l\}.$$

If $d(x,y)\le r_k$, there are about $k$ disjoint annuli between the two scales, and summing over them gives $|H_N(x)-H_N(y)|\le C k^{-1/2}$. This uniform modulus of continuity makes the sequence equicontinuous. If $\lambda>1$, a single annulus already gives $C\lambda^{-k/2}$, which is Hölder continuity.

Proposition 3.7 (Scalar limit). Along a subsequence of records, $H_N\to u$ locally uniformly on $U$ and $\mu_N\rightharpoonup\mu$. Here $u\colon U\to[0,1]$ is continuous and nonconstant, $\mu$ is finite, and $u$ is locally Hölder if $\lambda>1$. For compact $K\subset U$ and small $s$, uniformly in $x\in K$: $$\big(\osc_{B(x,s)}u\big)^2\ \le\ C\,\mu\big(B(x,C_\mu s)\big). \tag{3.6}$$ For each $0\lt t\lt 1$ there is a continuum $C_t\subset Q$ of diameter at least $d_1$ with $u=t$ on $C_t\cap U$, meeting every compact path in $U$ whose endpoint values straddle $t$. The transformed energy bounds of Lemma 3.5 hold along the subsequence.

Inequality (3.6) is the budget used in §5 and §6. On disjoint balls, squared oscillations of $u$ sum to at most $C\mu(Z)\lt \infty$.

3.5Transversality costs energy

Every contradiction in the paper goes through one finite-cell inequality.

Lemma 3.8 (A finite-cell level comparison). Let $f,w$ be continuous on an open $V$, $K\subset V$ compact, $I$ an interval. Suppose that for every $t\in I$ there is a continuum $D_t\subset K$ with $f=t$ on $D_t$ and $\osc_{D_t}w\ge b_0>0$. Then for large $n$ $$b_0\,|I|\ \le\ \sum_{q:\,B(q,2r_n)\cap K\ne\emptyset}\osc_{B(q,2r_n)}f\cdot\osc_{B(q,2r_n)}w\ \le\ \big(E_n(f;K)\,E_n(w;K)\big)^{1/2}. \tag{3.7}$$ In particular, these hypotheses cannot hold along sequences whose energies on $K$ both tend to $0$, if $b_0$ and $|I|$ stay bounded below.

The proof is short. The cells meeting $D_t$ cover it, so their $w$-ranges cover $w(D_t)$, and each such cell has $t$ in its $f$-range. Integrate over $t\in I$ and apply Cauchy–Schwarz. In words: if $w$ varies along the level sets of $f$, the two functions are transverse, and transversality costs energy. Functions with vanishing energy cannot be transverse. The paper applies this to $H_N$ and its group translates $H_N\circ g^{-1}$, which all have vanishing energy by Lemma 3.5. So the translates must be functionally dependent in a strong sense, and the group is too rich to allow that. Figure 5 shows the mechanism.

Lemma 3.9 (Stopping a continuum at a sphere). If a continuum $D$ meets $B(p,r)$ and contains $x\notin B(p,r)$, then $D$ has a subcontinuum through $x$ meeting the sphere of radius $r$ and avoiding the open ball. This is boundary bumping.

4Exponential records: comparing two magnifications

Suppose $\lambda>1$, so $u$ is locally Hölder. The plan has three steps. First, magnify the variation of $u$ to a nonconstant function $W$ on a punctured sphere. Second, use the approximants' level continua to show that any group transform of $W$ with a different puncture is constant wherever $W$ varies. Third, magnify a second time to get two such transforms that vary on the same two disks with disjoint ranges, which is impossible.

Lemma 4.1 (Hölder magnification). Let $f$ be locally Hölder on $\Omega$, nonconstant on a compact path in an open $O\Subset\Omega$, and fix $z_0$ away from $O$. There are expansions $h_i$ at $(x_i,k_i)$, punctures $p_i=h_iz_0\to b$, and normalizations $\sigma_i>0$ such that $F_i(z)=\big(f(h_i^{-1}z)-f(x_i)\big)/\sigma_i$ converges locally uniformly on $Z\setminus\{b\}$ to a nonconstant, locally Hölder $F$.
How the scales are chosen

Let $\delta(x)=\dist(x,Z\setminus O)$, fix $\eta>Q$, and set $A_k=\sup_{x\in O}\delta(x)^\eta\osc_{B(x,r_k)}f$. Packing gives $A_k\ge ca^{-Qk}$, and the Hölder bound gives $A_k\le Ca^{-\alpha_0k}$. Fix $\alpha\lt \alpha_0$ and take scales $k$ where $A_k$ is maximal over the remaining tail. These are records again, and at them $A_{k+j}\le a^{-\alpha j}A_k$. Expand at a near-maximizer $x_k$ and normalize by $\sigma_k=A_k/\delta_k^\eta$. Finer scales then have geometrically smaller normalized oscillation, which gives uniform Hölder bounds. The maximizing ball keeps oscillation at least $1/2$, so the limit is nonconstant. The comparison $A_k\ge ca^{-Qk}$ also forces $\delta_k/r_k\to\infty$, so the magnified window stays inside $O$.

Apply this to $u$ to get a limit $W$ on $Z\setminus\{b\}$. Lemma 4.2 diagonalizes. It chooses approximation indices $N_j$ so large that $f_j=(H_{N_j}\circ h_j^{-1}-u(x_j))/\sigma_j\to W$, and $E_{N_j}(f_j\circ g^{-1};K)\to0$ for every fixed $g$ and compact $K$ avoiding $gb$. The level continua $D_{j,t}=h_jC_{N_j,\tau_j(t)}$ of $f_j$ meet any variation path $P$ and come arbitrarily close to $b$. Each source continuum has diameter at least $d_1$, and its far part is crushed into the puncture.

Lemma 4.3 (Transform comparison). If $c=gb\neq b$ and $V=W\circ g^{-1}$, then $V(x)=V(b)$ for every $x\in\mathcal N(W)\setminus\{c\}$, where $\mathcal N(W)$ is the set where $W$ is not locally constant. More generally, if $p_s=sb\neq p_t$, then $W_t$ is constant (equal to $W_t(p_s)$) on $\mathcal N(W_s)\setminus\{p_t\}$.

Why. Suppose $V(x)\ne V(b)$ at a point $x$ where $W$ varies. Near $x$ there is a path $P$ along which $W$ varies, with $|V-V(b)|\ge 3\varepsilon$ there, and near $b$ we have $|V-V(b)|\le2\varepsilon$. Each level continuum of $f_j$ runs from $P$ into the small ball around $b$. Lemma 3.9 cuts it to a subcontinuum that avoids both punctures, and along it $w_j=f_j\circ g^{-1}$ changes by at least $\varepsilon$. Lemma 3.8 then gives $\varepsilon|I|\le(E(f_j)E(w_j))^{1/2}\to0$, a contradiction.

Lemma 4.4 (Stabilizer directions). For every $b\in Z$, the vertices of $E=\Stab_G(b)$ have at most two accumulation directions.

Thin triangles give linear growth, $\#\{q\in E:|q|\le t\}\le C(t+1)$. If $E$ accumulated anywhere other than $b$, it would contain a loxodromic element with endpoints $b,x'$. An element of $E$ moving $x'$ would then produce about $m^2$ distinct elements of length $O(m)$, contradicting linear growth. So $E$ fixes both endpoints and accumulates only there.

Proposition 4.5. The contradiction setup cannot have $\lambda>1$.

Pick a variation region $O$ for $W$ whose closure avoids $b$ and the at most two directions of $\Stab(b)$. Magnify $W$ again by Lemma 4.1 to get expansions $s_i$ with punctures $p_i=s_ib\to b_*$ and normalized limits $W_*$. Lemma 4.4 makes the punctures pairwise distinct along a subsequence: a repeat would put $v_iv^{-1}$ in $\Stab(b)$ and force the centers toward a stabilizer direction. Choose two disks $D_1,D_2$, away from $b_*$, on which $W_*$ varies and has disjoint ranges. For two distinct large indices $i,j$, $W_i$ varies in both disks. By (4.8), $W_j$ takes the single value $W_j(p_i)$ at a point of each disk. But $W_j$, like $W_*$, has disjoint ranges on the two disks. Contradiction.

5A probability flow in the subexponential case

Now assume $\limsup_j M(j)^{1/j}\le1$. Without Hölder continuity there is no clean magnified limit. The paper builds a tree of small balls carrying nested value intervals of $u$ and a probability flow, and shows that at arbitrarily deep levels much of the probability sits on nodes with two separated regions of variation.

5.1Nodes, candidates, good nodes

A node is a center $x$ in a compact $K$, a scale $R=r_k$, and an open interval $J=(j_-,j_+)\subset(0,1)$ contained in the range of $u$ on $B(x,c_0R)$, with $\Delta=|J|$. For every $t\in J$, the level continuum $C_t$ passes near $x$ and, having diameter at least $d_1$, crosses the annulus $A_x(R)=\{(A-1)R\le d(x,\cdot)\le(B_0+1)R\}$. Children have scale $r=\theta R$ with $\theta=a^{-D}$. The candidates are a maximal $r$-separated set $Y$ in the annulus, and $\#Y\le C_Ya^{QD}$. For $y\in Y$, let $I^{\rm in}_y$ and $I^{\rm out}_y$ be the ranges of $u$ on $B(y,2r)$ and $B(y,c_0r)$. Set $J_y=J\cap\operatorname{int}I^{\rm out}_y$ and $t_y=|J_y|/\Delta$.

A node is good if two candidates $y_\pm$ have inner ranges meeting the bottom and top quarters of $J$ by at least $\kappa\Delta$ ($\kappa=c_*a^{-QD}$), and outer ranges at distance at least $\Delta/4$. So a good node has two separated regions of variation.

5.2Transition probabilities

Fix $b_*=\tfrac12$, $\tau=\tfrac1{20}$, $\beta=\tau/(4(Q+1))$, $\gamma=\beta/2$. At each node the flow chooses an $Hr$-separated set of children with probabilities $p_y$ summing to $1$ and satisfying

$$\begin{aligned} &p_y\le a^{-\gamma D}t_y,\quad p_y\le a^{-b_*D}t_y^2 &&\text{at nodes that are not good,}\\ &p_y\le a^{-\gamma D}t_y,\quad p_y\le a^{\tau D}t_y^2 &&\text{at good nodes.} \end{aligned}$$

The linear bounds telescope along a branch and make deep probabilities small: $P_i\le a^{-\gamma Dn}\Delta_i/\Delta_0$. The quadratic bounds compare probability with squared interval length:

$$\Delta_i^2\ \ge\ \Delta_0^2\,P_i\,a^{D((b_*+\tau)m_i-\tau n)}, \tag{5.21}$$

where $m_i$ counts the not-good ancestors of node $i$. Each not-good step raises the ratio $\Delta^2/P$ by a factor $a^{b_*D}$. Each good step lowers it by at most $a^{\tau D}$.

Lemma 5.1 (Many children at a node that is not good). For large $D$, a node that is not good has at least $c_Ha^D$ mutually $Hr$-separated candidates with $t_y\ge1/8$.

At each radius $b\in[AR,B_0R]$, the inner intervals of nearby candidates cover $J$, so two of them meet the bottom and top quarters. If both had $t_y\lt 1/8$, their outer ranges would be $\Delta/4$ apart and the node would be good. So at each of about $a^D$ well-spaced radii some candidate has $t_y\ge1/8$. Uniform probabilities on these candidates satisfy the bad-node bounds.

Lemma 5.2 (Separated capacity). For large $D$, uniformly over all nodes, there is an $Hr$-separated $Y'\subset Y$ with $\sum_{y\in Y'}\min\{a^{-\gamma D}t_y,\ a^{\tau D}t_y^2\}\ge1$.
Proof idea (where subexponential growth is used)

Suppose the capacity is small. Call candidates with $t_y\ge a^{-(\tau+\gamma)D}$ large. Then $\sum_{\rm large}t_y\le C_Ha^{\gamma D}$, so large candidates can only cover a small part of the set of pairs (radius, level $t$). Use radial bins at an intermediate scale $s=a^{-D'}R$, $D'=\lceil\beta D\rceil$. The unmarked pairs have measure at least $ca^{D'}\Delta$. Detect them by modulus. At scale $s$ each unmarked level crossing expands to a macroscopic path, and admissible weights at relative depth $D-D'$ have mass $M(D-D')$. Integrating and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives $$\sum_{\rm small}t_y^2\ \ge\ c\,\frac{a^{-(Q-2)D'}}{M(D-D')}.$$ Subexponential growth gives $M(D-D')\le a^{\tau D/4}$, and $(Q-2)\beta\lt \tau/4$, so $\sum_{\rm small}a^{\tau D}t_y^2\ge ca^{\tau D/2}$. That is large, contradicting the assumption. The length $\Delta$ cancels, which is why one depth $D$ works for every node.

Every good node also records a pattern. Its two variation paths expand, under the node's fixed expansion, into an ordered pair of balls $(B_-,B_+)$ from a finite list that depends only on $D$. The $u\circ h^{-1}$-ranges of the two balls are $\Delta/4$ apart, and each ball contains a path with variation at least $\kappa\Delta$.

5.5The tree and the energy budget

Generation $n$ has scale $r_{k_0+Dn}$ and $H$-separated centers. The enlarged balls $B(x_i,C_\mu c_0R)$ are disjoint, so (3.6) gives a budget at every generation:

$$\sum_i\Delta_i^2\ \le\ E_0=C\mu(Z)\lt \infty. \tag{5.20}$$
Lemma 5.3 (Levels with substantial good mass). Infinitely many generations carry total probability at least $1/2$ on good nodes.

By (5.21), (5.20) and Jensen's inequality,

$$\frac{E_0}{\Delta_0^2}\ \ge\ \sum_iP_i\,a^{D((b_*+\tau)m_i-\tau n)}\ \ge\ a^{D\left((b_*+\tau)\sum_iP_im_i-\tau n\right)}.$$

So the average not-good mass over the first $n$ generations is at most $\tau/(b_*+\tau)+O(1/n)=\tfrac1{11}+O(1/n)$. If good mass were eventually below $1/2$, that average would tend to at least $1/2$.

6Same-depth expansions and the measure comparison

Proposition 6.1. The moduli $M(n)$ cannot be unbounded while satisfying $\limsup M(n)^{1/n}\le1$.

Take one of the generations $n$ with good mass at least $1/2$. Write $k=k_0+Dn$, $R=a^{-k}$, and let $p_i=h_iz_0$ be the punctures of the nodes' fixed expansions.

6.1A probability class with separated punctures

Bounded multiplicity (Lemma 6.2). The puncture balls $B(p_i,CR)$ have bounded overlap. Nearby punctures force the inverse words to agree, and $H$-separation bounds how many centers can share a word.

A class. Discard nodes with $P_i\lt a^{-2Qk}$. Then fix one pattern and one dyadic probability class $I$ with $P_*\le P_i\le2P_*$ and $\sum_{i\in I}P_i\ge c/k$. With $N=|I|$ this gives $NP_*\ge c/(2k)$, and $N\to\infty$. Put $\zeta=\gamma/Q$, $m=\lfloor\zeta n\rfloor$, $l=Dm$, so that $Ql\le\gamma Dn$.

Lemma 6.3 (Probability near a puncture). For every node $i$ of generation $n$, $\displaystyle\sum_{j:\ d(p_j,p_i)\le a^{-l}}P_j\le Ca^{-\gamma Dm}$.

Split the inverse word $v_j^{-1}=w_ju_j'$ with $|w_j|=l$. A puncture close to $p_i$ pins down the first $l$ letters up to bounded ambiguity. The descendant's center lies near its ancestor, which pins down the last $k-l$ letters. So each ancestor at generation $n-m$ has boundedly many such descendants, each of probability at most $a^{-\gamma Dm}$ times the ancestor's. As a result all but a vanishing fraction of the class is far from each $p_i$, and there are at least $cN^2$ far pairs with $d_{ij}=d(p_i,p_j)>a^{-l}$, for which $R/d_{ij}\le a^{-(k-l)}\to0$.

6.2The pair alternative

Lemma 6.4 (Pair alternative). Every far pair at a large generation has an endpoint $i$ with $\osc_{h_i^{-1}E_j}u\ \ge\ \tfrac{\kappa}{16}\Delta_i$, where $E_j=B(p_j,A_hR)$.

Write $F_i=u\circ h_i^{-1}$. If $F_j$ oscillates by at least $\Delta_j/16$ on $E_i$, then $j$ pays. Otherwise one of $i$'s pattern balls $B_s$ has $F_j$-range far from $F_j(E_i)$. Suppose $F_i$ is also nearly constant on $E_j$. In $i$'s chart, the level continua of the approximants then run from the variation path $\eta\subset B_s$ to the puncture ball $E_i$ while avoiding $E_j$. Along each of them $F_{j,N}$ changes by at least $\Delta_j/32$. Lemma 3.8 with vanishing energies gives a contradiction, so $F_i$ oscillates on $E_j$ and $i$ pays. This is Lemma 4.3 again, applied at a single depth instead of in the limit.

6.3Cost balls and total multiplicity

Pull the paying neighborhood back to the source. With $z_{ij}=h_i^{-1}p_j$, the expansion formula gives $D_{z_{ij}}\asymp R/d_{ij}$ and

$$h_i^{-1}E_j\ \subseteq\ B(z_{ij},s_{ij}),\qquad s_{ij}=C_2\frac{R^2}{d_{ij}^2}. \tag{6.9}$$

Let $D_{ij}=B(z_{ij},C_\mu s_{ij})$ be its fixed enlargement. By (3.6) a paying $i$ gives $\mu(D_{ij})\ge c\kappa^2\Delta_i^2$. The scales separate: $C_\mu s_{ij}/D_{z_{ij}}=O(R/d_{ij})\to0$. So the enlargement still maps into a ball of radius $C_3R$ around $p_j$, and for fixed $i$ the cost balls have bounded multiplicity in $j$. All cost balls of node $i$ sit in $B(x_i,C_4Ra^l)$, so any point lies in at most $Ca^{Ql}$ of them across different $i$. Therefore

$$\sum_{\{i,j\}\ \text{far}}\mu(D_{ij})\ \le\ C\,a^{Ql}\,\mu(Z). \tag{6.13}$$

On the other hand, $\Delta_i\ge\Delta_0a^{\gamma Dn}P_i$ by the linear bounds, and there are at least $cN^2$ far pairs, so

$$\sum\mu(D_{ij})\ \ge\ cN^2\kappa^2\Delta_0^2a^{2\gamma Dn}P_*^2\ \ge\ c'k^{-2}a^{2\gamma Dn}. \tag{6.14}$$

Since $Ql\le\gamma Dn$, comparing the two bounds gives $a^{\gamma Dn}\le Ck^2$, which fails because $k=k_0+Dn$ grows only linearly. This rules out the subexponential case.

7Completion of the proof

If $M(n)$ were unbounded, the record selection of §3 would give either exponential records, excluded by Proposition 4.5, or subexponential growth, excluded by Proposition 6.1. Hence $\sup_nM(n)\lt \infty$. Theorem 2.5 uniformizes $\partial G$ to the round sphere, and Proposition 2.6 gives the proper cocompact action on $\Hyp^3$ with finite kernel. $\blacksquare$

The shape of the argument Unbounded modulus gives a nonconstant function $u$ that is a limit of approximants whose energy vanishes at every fixed group transformation. Lemma 3.8 says such functions cannot be transverse to each other. Expansions with distinct punctures make them transverse. When $\lambda>1$, Hölder magnification produces this transversality in a clean limit (§4). When $\lambda=1$, there is no limit, so the paper counts: a probability flow finds many nodes with separated variation at one depth (§5), and comparing their expansions in pairs costs more $\mu$-mass than exists (§6).

8Consequences

Corollary 8.1. If $G$ is torsion-free hyperbolic with $\partial G\cong S^2$, then $G$ is the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold, possibly nonorientable.

Point stabilizers of a proper action are finite, hence trivial, so the action is free and $\Hyp^3/G$ is the manifold.

Corollary 8.2 (Virtual structure). Such a $G$ has a finite-index subgroup isomorphic to $\pi_1(\Sigma)\rtimes\Z$ for a closed orientable surface $\Sigma$. In particular it has positive virtual first Betti number. Moreover $G$ is virtually compact special and residually finite.

These follow from Agol's virtual fibering and virtual specialness theorems, applied to the orientation cover, and from Bergeron–Wise's cubulation of closed hyperbolic 3-manifold groups using Kahn–Marković surfaces. Residual finiteness of $G$ itself follows from that of the index-$\le2$ subgroup $G^+$.

In the torsion-free case, the theorem says that the coarse topology of a group at infinity decides whether it is a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold group. This ties together Wall's question on 3-dimensional Poincaré duality groups, hyperbolic geometrization, and the virtual-Haken circle of results. Each of these conclusions depends on the proof surviving independent checking.
