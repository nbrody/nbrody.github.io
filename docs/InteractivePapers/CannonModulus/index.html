Cannon's Conjecture

OpenAI · A Modulus Proof of Cannon's Conjecture, preprint, September 23, 2026

A hyperbolic group whose boundary is a topological $2$-sphere acts properly and cocompactly by isometries on hyperbolic $3$-space. The preprint proves this by showing that one number stays bounded: $M(n)$, the cost of blocking every large path on the boundary with cells of size $a^{-n}$. Classical uniformization then finishes. If $M(n)$ were unbounded, the proof builds a continuous function on the boundary whose energy vanishes, and shows that two of its group translates would have to cut across each other's level sets, which costs energy they do not have.

Before the paper background

Hyperbolic groups and their boundaries

Fix a finite symmetric generating set of a group $G$ and draw its Cayley graph $\Gamma$: a vertex for each element, an edge of length one from $g$ to $gs$ for each generator $s$. The group is hyperbolic if geodesic triangles in $\Gamma$ are uniformly thin: each side lies within a fixed distance $\delta$ of the other two. Free groups, fundamental groups of closed negatively curved manifolds, and cocompact Kleinian groups are hyperbolic.

The boundary $\partial G$ is the set of geodesic rays from the identity, two rays being identified when they stay a bounded distance apart. Two rays that agree for a long time end at nearby boundary points. The Gromov product $(y|z)_e$ measures how long the rays to $y$ and $z$ travel together, and a visual metric makes this exact up to constants: $d(y,z)\approx a^{-(y|z)_e}$ for some $a\gt1$. A free group has a Cantor set as boundary, a surface group a circle, and a cocompact Kleinian group the round $2$-sphere, on which the group acts by Möbius transformations.

Cannon's conjecture

Cannon conjectured the converse of the last example: if $\partial G$ is homeomorphic to the $2$-sphere, then $G$ is, up to finite kernel, a cocompact Kleinian group. The topology of the boundary would then determine the geometry of the group. Bonk and Kleiner (2002) reduced this to a question about the metric on the boundary: the action is geometric as soon as the visual metric is quasisymmetrically equivalent to the round one, that is, as soon as it distorts shapes by a bounded amount at every scale.

Combinatorial modulus

The tool for comparing a metric sphere with the round one is modulus, the discrete version of extremal length. Cover the sphere by small cells. Given a family of paths, assign each cell a nonnegative weight so that every path in the family meets cells of total weight at least $1$. The modulus of the family is the least possible sum of squared weights: how expensive it is to block all the paths. On the round sphere, the modulus of the paths of diameter at least a fixed $d_0$ stays bounded as the cells shrink: weights about the size of a cell suffice, and there are about $(\text{cell size})^{-2}$ cells. Cannon's combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem (1994) and the Bonk–Kleiner and Bourdon–Kleiner criteria turn bounded modulus back into a round structure.

Conformal structures

A homeomorphism of the sphere is quasiconformal if it maps infinitesimal circles to ellipses of bounded eccentricity. At a point, an ellipse up to scale is a point of the hyperbolic plane (positive definite quadratic forms modulo scalars), and a linear map acts on these by isometries. Sullivan's idea: if a group acts on the sphere with uniformly bounded distortion, the images of the round structure at each point form a bounded set in the hyperbolic plane, and its circumcenter is an invariant measurable conformal structure. The measurable Riemann mapping theorem straightens it, and the group becomes Möbius.

1.Introduction

Theorem 1.1 (Cannon's conjecture). Let $G$ be a hyperbolic group with $\partial G$ homeomorphic to $\Sphere$. There is a homomorphism $\rho:G\to\Isom(\HH^3)$ for which the action on $\HH^3$ is proper and cocompact. Its kernel is finite.

Proper means that only finitely many group elements bring a compact set back to meet itself; cocompact means that translates of one compact set cover $\HH^3$. The image may contain orientation-reversing isometries, so no orientation needs to be chosen. For a torsion-free group the quotient $\HH^3/\rho(G)$ is a closed hyperbolic $3$-manifold, possibly nonorientable (Corollary 8.1).

History and the classical reduction

The paper places itself on the analytic route:

  • Cannon (1994): the combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem recovers conformal coordinates on a sphere from discrete annular moduli.
  • Cannon–Swenson (1998) state the group-boundary conjecture and relate a geometric action to conformality of the boundary's coverings; Cannon–Floyd–Parry (1999) show it suffices to bound discrete moduli of annuli from below around every point.
  • Bonk–Kleiner (2002): a metric sphere with suitable modulus bounds is quasisymmetric to the round sphere. The paper uses the exact form in Bourdon–Kleiner (2013), Corollary 3.5: on an approximately self-similar sphere, a uniform bound on the combinatorial $2$-modulus of paths of a fixed small diameter gives a quasi-Möbius parametrization by the round sphere.
  • Bonk–Kleiner (2005) proved the conjecture when the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension (Pansu's invariant) is attained. Attainment is a hypothesis there.

The algebraic route goes through surface subgroups and cube complexes: Kahn–Marković, Bergeron–Wise, Agol, Marković's criterion, Haïssinsky's planar-boundary criterion, and the drilling theorem of Groves, Haïssinsky, Manning, Osajda, Sisto and Walsh. Those results give the consequences in §8 once a Kleinian realization is known. The present proof takes the analytic route and proves the modulus bound directly.

The proof in outline

  1. Reduction (§2). Cover the boundary $Z=\partial G$ at scale $a^{-n}$ by balls on a separated net. Let $M(n)$ be the least sum of squared weights blocking every path of diameter at least $d_0$. If $\sup_nM(n)\lt\infty$, Theorem 2.5 uniformizes $Z$ and Proposition 2.6 builds the action.
  2. A scalar limit (§3). Suppose $M(n)$ is unbounded. At record scales, crossing duality gives weights of energy $O(1/M(N))$ that block crossings of a rectangle. Shortest-path costs turn them into continuous functions $H_N$ whose level sets are separating continua. Their limit is a nonconstant function $u$ with a finite measure $\mu$ controlling its oscillation, (3.6). The approximants keep vanishing local energy after any fixed group translation.
  3. Two branches. If $M(n)$ grows exponentially, $u$ is Hölder, and magnifying its variation twice gives two transforms that are forced to be constant on each other's variation, a contradiction (§4). If $M(n)$ grows subexponentially, a tree of small balls carries a probability flow that puts definite mass on nodes with two separated variations (§5); comparing many such nodes in pairs charges more measure than their overlap allows (§6).

Both contradictions come from one inequality, Lemma 3.8: two functions whose level sets cut across each other cannot both have small local energy.

2.Boundary geometry and the modulus criterion

Let $\Gamma$ be the Cayley graph, $e$ its identity vertex, $|v|$ the distance from $e$ to $v$, and $(y|z)_o=\tfrac12\bigl(d(o,y)+d(o,z)-d(y,z)\bigr)$ the Gromov product. Write $Z=\partial G$. A visual metric $d$ on $Z$ satisfies

$$C_v^{-1}a^{-(y|z)_e}\le d(y,z)\le C_va^{-(y|z)_e},\qquad y\ne z,\tag{2.1}$$

for some $a\gt1$. Scales are $r_k=a^{-k}$.

Expansions

Left multiplication by the inverse of a vertex far out along a ray magnifies a small ball of the boundary to macroscopic size. The point of the next lemma is that it also says what happens to everything else: the complement of the ball is squeezed toward one point, the puncture.

Lemma 2.1 (Expansion formula). Let $v$ be the depth-$k$ vertex on a based ray to $x\in Z$, put $h=v^{-1}$ and $R=r_k$, and set $D_y=\max\{R,d(x,y)\}$. There are constants $c_E,C_E\gt0$, independent of these choices, with $$c_E\frac{R\,d(y,z)}{D_yD_z}\le d(hy,hz)\le C_E\frac{R\,d(y,z)}{D_yD_z}.\tag{2.2}$$ So on $B(x,CR)$ the map $h$ is bi-Lipschitz with scale factor $R^{-1}$. If $d(x,z_0)\ge\varepsilon_*\gt0$, $R\lt\varepsilon_*/2$ and $p=hz_0$, then $$d(hy,p)\lesssim R/D_y\ \ (y\in Z),\qquad d(hy,p)\asymp R/D_y\ \ (d(x,y)\le\varepsilon_*/2),\tag{2.3}$$ $$(v^{-1}|p)_e\ge k-C(\varepsilon_*).\tag{2.4}$$

The proof is a change of base point in the Gromov product,

$$(y|z)_v=(y|z)_e+k-(v|y)_e-(v|z)_e+O(1),$$

together with $a^{-(v|y)_e}\asymp D_y$. Exponentiating gives (2.2). The denominator $D_yD_z$ is what makes the formula useful later: it controls the image of the whole sphere, not only of the ball. Points far from $x$ land within $O(R)$ of the puncture $p$, by (2.3).

Three routine facts follow from thin triangles and are used throughout.

Lemma 2.2 (Prefixes). Every vertex $v$ lies a bounded distance from a depth-$|v|$ vertex on a based ray, and (2.2) holds for $h=v^{-1}$ and $R=r_{|v|}$ with uniform constants. Moreover: (i) if $d(\xi,\eta)\le Cr_j$, depth-$j$ vertices on rays to $\xi$ and $\eta$ are a bounded distance apart; (ii) if $|v|=k$ and $(v|\xi)_e\ge k-C$, every geodesic from $e$ to $v$ stays a bounded distance from a ray to $\xi$ through depth $k$.
Lemma 2.3 (Packing). There are $Q\ge2$ and $C_P\lt\infty$ such that an $s$-separated subset of a ball of radius $t$ has at most $C_P(t/s)^Q$ points, for $0\lt s\le t\le\diam Z$. In particular $Z$ is doubling.
Lemma 2.4 (Controlled paths). There is $L\ge1$ such that any two points of $\overline B(x,s)$ are joined by a path in $B(x,Ls)$. Any two points of a continuum can be joined by a path in any prescribed open neighbourhood of it.

Lemma 2.4 is the first place the hypothesis $\partial G\cong\Sphere$ enters. Expand at $x$ by Lemma 2.1; the two points land a definite distance from the puncture; on a topological sphere they can be joined while avoiding a neighbourhood of the puncture; pulling back puts the path in $B(x,Ls)$. Because expansions exist at every scale, the constant $L$ is uniform.

The whole action is also uniformly quasi-Möbius: the base-change factors cancel in cross-ratios, so one constant $C$ bounds the distortion of $\frac{d(x,z)d(y,w)}{d(x,w)d(y,z)}$ by every $g\in G$.

The classical reduction

Choose a maximal $r_n$-separated set $T_n\subset Z$; its cells are the balls $B(q,2r_n)$, $q\in T_n$. For a family $\cF$ of paths,

$$\Mod_2(\cF,T_n)=\min_{\omega\ge0}\Bigl\{\sum_{q\in T_n}\omega(q)^2:\ \sum_{B(q,2r_n)\cap\pi\ne\varnothing}\omega(q)\ge1\ \text{for every }\pi\in\cF\Bigr\}.$$

Fix a small $d_0\gt0$ and take the macroscopic paths:

$$\cF_0=\{\pi:\diam\pi\ge d_0\},\qquad M(n)=\Mod_2(\cF_0,T_n).\tag{2.5}$$

The constant $d_0$ is chosen so that a path of diameter at least $R/2$ in $\overline B(x,3R)$ becomes macroscopic under a scale-$R$ expansion, which (2.2) allows. All-one weights are admissible and any single path gives a lower bound, so $0\lt M(n)\lt\infty$.

Theorem 2.5 (Bonk–Kleiner; Bourdon–Kleiner). If $\sup_nM(n)\lt\infty$, then $(Z,d)$ is quasi-Möbius homeomorphic to the round sphere.

This is Bourdon–Kleiner's Corollary 3.5, derived from Bonk–Kleiner's Theorem 10.4. The paper checks its hypotheses: approximate self-similarity (each ball, rescaled by its radius, is uniformly bi-Lipschitz to an open subset) is Lemma 2.1, and the cells form a fixed $\kappa$-approximation at each dyadic scale, with modulus exactly $M(n)$. No Coxeter-group assumption is involved.

Proposition 2.6 (From the boundary to hyperbolic space). If $(Z,d)$ is quasi-Möbius homeomorphic to the round sphere, then $G$ admits a proper cocompact isometric action on $\HH^3$ with finite kernel.
How the action is built
  1. Uniform quasiconformality. Conjugate the action to the round sphere by the uniformizing map $\psi$. The cross-ratio bound, with a fourth point held away, bounds the infinitesimal distortion of every $f=\psi g\psi^{-1}$ by one constant. By the metric characterization of quasiconformality (Heinonen–Koskela), every $f$ is quasiconformal with a common bound, orientation-reversing ones after a reflection.
  2. An invariant structure. Off a $G$-invariant null set, each $f$ has a nonsingular differential and the chain rule holds. At such a point $x$, the conformal structures form a hyperbolic plane $\mathcal C_x$, and pullback by any invertible linear map, of either orientation, is an isometry. The orbit $\{f^*q_{\rm round}(x)\}$ is bounded and contains the round form; its unique circumcenter $q(x)$ is measurable, bounded, and invariant, because pullback permutes the orbit (Sullivan, with Tukia's circumcenter).
  3. Straightening. The measurable Riemann mapping theorem gives a quasiconformal $F$ with $F^*q_{\rm round}=q$. Each $FfF^{-1}$ is then conformal or anticonformal, so Möbius or anti-Möbius, and extends to an isometry of $\HH^3$.
  4. Proper and cocompact. On ordered distinct triples of boundary points the action is proper and cocompact, by expansions. The map from triples of $\Sphere$ to $\HH^3$ (via the stabilizer of a triple, of order two) is proper and equivariant, so both properties descend. The kernel fixes a point, so it is finite.

So the theorem comes down to one estimate: $\sup_nM(n)\lt\infty$. The rest of the paper assumes it fails and reaches a contradiction.

3.A scalar limit from unbounded modulus

Assume $M(n)$ is unbounded. This section produces the object both contradictions are about: a nonconstant continuous function $u$ on an open part of $Z$, a finite measure $\mu$ controlling its oscillation, and approximants $H_N$ whose local energy tends to zero even after a fixed group element moves them.

Record scales and crossing duality

If $\limsup M(n)^{1/n}\gt1$, choose $1\lt\lambda\lt\limsup M(n)^{1/n}$; otherwise put $\lambda=1$. Either way $M(n)/\lambda^n$ is unbounded, so there are record indices $N$ with $M(N)\to\infty$ and

$$M(N-l)\le\lambda^{-l}M(N)\qquad(0\le l\le N).\tag{3.1}$$

A record scale is one where the modulus is larger than at every coarser scale, after discounting by $\lambda$. The annular estimate (3.5) will compare scale $N$ with scale $N-l$; records make that comparison favourable.

Lemma 3.1 (Localization to a rectangle). There are coordinate rectangles $\cQ=[0,1]^2$ and $\Qhat=[0,1]\times[-1,2]$, with $\cQ\subset\Qhat\subset Z$, and a subsequence of record indices such that the left-to-right crossing modulus of $\cQ$ is at least $cM(N)$, with $c\gt0$ independent of $N$.

A macroscopic path must cross one of finitely many coordinate strips, so one strip carries a fixed fraction of the modulus. The edge $y=0$ is the top of $\cQ$; $[0,1]\times[-1,0]$ is the top band and $[0,1]\times[1,2]$ the bottom band. Coordinates serve only topology and interpolation; all distances are visual.

Lemma 3.2 (Crossings and blockers). In a closed coordinate rectangle: (i) every continuum meeting both lateral edges meets every top-to-bottom path; (ii) every compact set meeting all top-to-bottom paths contains a continuum meeting both lateral edges.

Part (i) is the Jordan curve theorem. Part (ii) is proved by a grid argument: mark the squares meeting the blocker, label $A$ the unmarked squares reachable from the top and $B$ the rest, and follow the interfaces between labels. Each lateral side has an odd number of interface ends, so some interface component runs from side to side, and the $B$ squares along it are marked squares forming a lateral crossing. Figure 3 runs this argument.

Lemma 3.3 (Crossing dual weights). For each retained $N$ there are nonnegative weights $\rho_q=\rho_q^{(N)}$, $q\in T_N$, admissible for top-to-bottom crossings of $\cQ$, such that $$\sum_{q\in T_N}\rho_q^2\le\frac{C}{M(N)}.\tag{3.2}$$

Large modulus in one direction means small modulus in the other. The proof is finite-dimensional: let $P$ be the point of least norm in the convex hull of the cell-incidence vectors of left-to-right crossings. Minimality gives $\langle P,v\rangle\ge\|P\|^2$ for every crossing vector $v$, so the left-to-right modulus is exactly $\|P\|^{-2}$. By Lemma 3.2(i) every top-to-bottom path meets every left-to-right one, so its incidence vector has inner product at least $1$ with each $v$, hence with $P$: the weights $\rho=P$ block top-to-bottom paths, with energy $\|P\|^2\le C/M(N)$. This is discrete extremal-length duality (Cannon–Floyd–Parry; Albin–Poggi-Corradini), reproved for these cells.

Continuous functions with separating levels

The weights $\rho$ give every path $\pi\subset\Qhat$ a cost $\ell_\rho(\pi)=\sum_{B(q,2r_N)\cap\pi\ne\varnothing}\rho_q$. Let $b_N(x)$ be the least cost of a path from the top edge to $x$ inside $\cQ$. The raw function is $\min\{1,b_N\}$, extended by $0$ on the top band and $1$ on the bottom band; admissibility makes it $1$ on the bottom edge. Along every path, the raw values at the ends differ by at most the path's cost.

Interpolating the raw values on a fine triangulation gives a continuous $U_N^0:\Qhat\to[0,1]$. Continuity alone does not make a level set separate: a level of $U_N^0$ can have islands. Two path envelopes repair this:

$$F_N(x)=\inf_{\pi:x\to\mathrm{top}}\max_\pi U_N^0,\qquad H_N(x)=\sup_{\pi:x\to\mathrm{bottom}}\inf_\pi F_N,$$

with paths in $\Qhat$ ending in the indicated band. $F_N(x)$ is the lowest height at which $x$ can escape to the top; $H_N(x)$ is the highest height from which $x$ can descend to the bottom without rising above $F_N$.

Lemma 3.4 (Regularization and level continua). $F_N$ and $H_N$ are continuous, with the prescribed band values, and for every path $K\subset\Qhat$, $$\osc_KH_N\le\osc_KF_N\le\osc_KU_N^0.\tag{3.3}$$ For every $0\lt t\lt1$, the set $\{H_N=t\}\cap\cQ$ contains a lateral crossing continuum $C_{N,t}$ of diameter at least a fixed $d_1\gt0$. Every path in $\Qhat$ whose endpoint values strictly straddle $t$ meets $C_{N,t}$.

The envelopes never increase oscillation, so the energy estimates for $U_N^0$ pass to $H_N$. What they add is topology: every point with $H_N\gt t$ joins the bottom band inside $\{H_N\gt t\}$, and every point with $H_N\lt t$ joins the top inside $\{H_N\lt t\}$. A path straddling $t$ can therefore be completed to a top-to-bottom path whose added pieces avoid the level, and that path meets the lateral continuum by Lemma 3.2. The paper's first figure draws this; Figure 5 computes it.

Oscillation and annular estimates

Let $U$ be the interior of $\Qhat$ in $Z$. For a continuous $f$ near a compact $K$ and $J\ge2$, the local oscillation energy at scale $n$ is

$$\cE_n^{(J)}(f;K)=\sum_{q\in T_n,\ B(q,2r_n)\cap K\ne\varnothing}\bigl(\osc_{\overline B(q,Jr_n)}f\bigr)^2.$$
Lemma 3.5 (Local oscillation). For fixed compact $K\subset U$ and $J\gt0$, uniformly for $x\in K$ and large $N$, $$\osc_{\overline B(x,Jr_N)}H_N\le\sum_{q\in T_N,\ d(q,x)\le C(J)r_N}\rho_q.\tag{3.4}$$ Consequently, for every fixed $g\in G$, compact $K'\subset gU$ and $J\ge2$, $$\cE_N^{(J)}(H_N\circ g^{-1};K')\le\frac{C(g,K',J)}{M(N)}\longrightarrow0.$$

The oscillation of $H_N$ on a small ball is paid for by the weights nearby. Squaring and summing, the local energy is at most a constant times $\sum\rho_q^2\le C/M(N)$, and a fixed group element changes only the constant. This is the property both contradictions use: every fixed translate of $H_N$ has vanishing local energy.

Lemma 3.6 (Annular estimate). For every compact $K\subset U$ there are $R_*\gt0$ and $m_0$ such that if $x\in K$, $R=r_l\le R_*$ and $N-l\ge m_0$, then any two points joined by a path in $B(x,R/2)$ have $H_N$-values differing by $\Delta$ with $$\Delta^2\le CM(N-l)\sum_{q\in T_N,\ R/4\le d(q,x)\le4R}\rho_q^2.\tag{3.5}$$

For each value $t$ between the two, the level continuum $C_{N,t}$ meets the short path and leaves $B(x,2R)$, so it crosses the annulus $R\le d(x,\cdot)\le2R$. Expanding at $x$ by Lemma 2.1 makes these crossings macroscopic at scale $N-l$, where macroscopic weights of energy $M(N-l)$ block them. Integrating over $t$ and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives (3.5). This is where the records enter: by (3.1), $M(N-l)\le\lambda^{-l}M(N)$.

The limiting function and measure

The measures $\mu_N=M(N)\sum_q\rho_q^2\delta_q$ have bounded mass by (3.2).

Proposition 3.7 (Scalar limit). Along a subsequence of record indices, $H_N\to u$ locally uniformly on $U$ and $\mu_N\rightharpoonup\mu$, where $u:U\to[0,1]$ is continuous and nonconstant and $\mu$ is finite. The function $u$ is $0$ and $1$ on the interiors of the two bands, and locally Hölder if $\lambda\gt1$. For every compact $K\subset U$ and small $s\gt0$, uniformly for $x\in K$, $$\bigl(\osc_{\overline B(x,s)}u\bigr)^2\le C\mu\bigl(\overline B(x,C_\mu s)\bigr).\tag{3.6}$$ For each $0\lt t\lt1$ there is a continuum $C_t\subset\cQ$ of diameter at least $d_1$, with $u=t$ on $C_t\cap U$, meeting every compact path in $U$ whose endpoint values strictly straddle $t$. The fixed-transform energy bounds of Lemma 3.5 hold along the subsequence.

Summing (3.5) over about $k$ disjoint annuli around two points at distance $r_k$ gives $|H_N(x)-H_N(y)|\le Ck^{-1/2}$: equicontinuity, so Arzelà–Ascoli applies. When $\lambda\gt1$ a single annulus gives $C\lambda^{-k/2}$, a Hölder bound with exponent $\log\lambda/(2\log a)$. The band values make $u$ nonconstant.

Two comparison tools

The scalar limit is used three ways: its level continua $C_t$ cross annuli; (3.6) turns oscillation on a small ball into $\mu$-mass of a larger one; and the approximants $H_N$, with vanishing energy after every fixed transformation, compare two transforms. The comparison is the next lemma, a finite-cell statement that does not depend on the construction.

Lemma 3.8 (A finite-cell level comparison). Let $f,w$ be continuous on an open $V\subset Z$, $K\subset V$ compact and $I\subset\RR$ an interval. Suppose that for every $t\in I$ there is a continuum $D_t\subset K$ with $f=t$ on $D_t$ and $\osc_{D_t}w\ge b_0\gt0$. Then for large $n$, $$b_0|I|\le\sum_{q\in T_n,\ B(q,2r_n)\cap K\ne\varnothing}\osc_{\overline B(q,2r_n)}f\,\osc_{\overline B(q,2r_n)}w.\tag{3.7}$$ Consequently these hypotheses cannot hold along sequences with both local oscillation energies on $K$ tending to zero, if $b_0$ and $|I|$ stay bounded below.

For each $t$, the range of $w$ on $D_t$ has length at least $b_0$ and is covered by the ranges of $w$ on the cells meeting $D_t$, each of which has $t$ in its $f$-range. Integrating over $t\in I$ gives (3.7). By Cauchy–Schwarz the right side is at most $\cE(f)^{1/2}\cE(w)^{1/2}$, so if $f$'s level sets cut across $w$'s variation, the two energies cannot both vanish. Figure 6 computes both sides.

Lemma 3.9 (Stopping a continuum at a sphere). Let $D$ be a continuum, $p\in Z$ and $r\gt0$. If $x\in D\setminus\overline B(p,r)$ and $D\cap B(p,r)\ne\varnothing$, then $D$ contains a subcontinuum through $x$ meeting the radius-$r$ sphere about $p$ and avoiding $B(p,r)$.

A point-set fact: the component of $x$ in $D\setminus B(p,r)$ must reach the sphere, or it would be clopen in $D$. It is used to cut level continua off before they reach a puncture.

4.Exponential records: comparing two magnifications

Suppose $\lambda\gt1$, so $u$ is locally Hölder. Magnify its variation to get a nonconstant function $W$ on the sphere minus one puncture. The level continua force every transform of $W$ with a different puncture to be constant wherever $W$ varies. A second magnification produces two transforms that must vary on the same two disks with disjoint ranges, which is impossible.

Magnifying variation

Lemma 4.1 (Hölder magnification). Let $f$ be locally Hölder on an open $\Omega\subset Z$, $O$ open with $\overline O\subset\Omega$ containing a compact path on which $f$ is nonconstant, and $z_0$ with $\dist(z_0,O)\gt0$. There are depths $k_i\to\infty$, centers $x_i\in O$, numbers $\sigma_i\gt0$ and expansions $h_i=v_i^{-1}$ at $(x_i,k_i)$ such that, after a subsequence, $p_i=h_iz_0\to b$, $\dist(x_i,Z\setminus O)/r_{k_i}\to\infty$, every compact subset of $Z\setminus\{b\}$ eventually lies in $h_i\Omega$, and $$F_i(z)=\frac{f(h_i^{-1}z)-f(x_i)}{\sigma_i}\tag{4.1}$$ converges locally uniformly on $Z\setminus\{b\}$ to a nonconstant locally Hölder function $F$.

The scales are chosen where a weighted oscillation $A_k=\sup_x\delta(x)^\eta\osc_{\overline B(x,r_k)}f$ is maximal over its tail; (4.2)–(4.5) in the proof record the bounds $ca^{-Qk}\le A_k\le Ca^{-\alpha_0k}$ and the resulting uniform Hölder control of the rescaled functions. The Hölder bound keeps the magnifications from degenerating.

A common mesh and escaping level continua

Apply Lemma 4.1 to $u$ and call the limit $W$, with puncture $b$.

Lemma 4.2 (Diagonal approximants). After thinning, there are scalar indices $N_j$ such that $f_j(z)=\bigl(H_{N_j}(h_j^{-1}z)-u(x_j)\bigr)/\sigma_j$ converges locally uniformly to $W$ on $Z\setminus\{b\}$, and for every fixed $g\in G$, compact $K\subset Z\setminus\{gb\}$ and $J\ge2$, $$\cE_{N_j}^{(J)}(f_j\circ g^{-1};K)\longrightarrow0.\tag{4.6}$$ If a compact path $P\subset Z\setminus\{b\}$ has $W$-endpoint values strictly straddling a compact interval $I$, then for large $j$ and every $t\in I$ there is a continuum $D_{j,t}$ with $f_j=t$ on it, meeting $P$, and $\sup_t\dist(b,D_{j,t})\to0$.

The order of choices matters: each expansion and its normalization are fixed first, and only then is $N_j$ taken large enough to make the energy small. The level continua are images of the large continua $C_{N,t}$, so after expansion they reach all the way to the puncture.

Comparison across punctures

Let $\cN(F)$ be the set of points where a continuous function $F$ is not locally constant.

Lemma 4.3 (Transform comparison). If $c=gb\ne b$ and $V=W\circ g^{-1}$, then $$V(x)=V(b)\qquad\bigl(x\in\cN(W)\setminus\{c\}\bigr).\tag{4.7}$$ More generally, for $W_s=W\circ s^{-1}$ and $p_s=sb$, if $p_s\ne p_t$, $$W_t(x)=W_t(p_s)\qquad\bigl(x\in\cN(W_s)\setminus\{p_t\}\bigr).\tag{4.8}$$

Suppose $V(x)\ne V(b)$ at a point where $W$ varies. Near $x$, the level continua $D_{j,t}$ of $f_j$ cross a small variation path of $W$ and run off to $b$, where $V$ is close to $V(b)$. So the translate $w_j=f_j\circ g^{-1}$ oscillates by a definite amount along each level continuum of $f_j$. Lemma 3.8 then bounds $\varepsilon|I|$ by the product of the two local energies, and both tend to zero by (4.6). This is the situation of Figure 6 with two different punctures.

The second magnification

Lemma 4.4 (Stabilizer directions). For every $b\in Z$, the vertices of $E=\Stab_G(b)$ have at most two accumulation directions.

The proof first shows linear growth, $\#\{q\in E:|q|\le t\}\le C'(t+1)$, which is (4.9). If $E$ accumulated anywhere other than $b$, it would contain a loxodromic element, and every element of $E$ would fix both of its endpoints.

Proposition 4.5. The contradiction setup cannot have $\lambda\gt1$.
Proof idea

Choose a variation region $O$ for $W$ away from $b$ and the at most two directions of $\Stab(b)$; one exists because a function locally constant off finitely many points of a sphere is constant. Magnify $W$ again by Lemma 4.1, with expansions $s_i$ and punctures $p_i=s_ib$. The punctures can be taken pairwise distinct: repeats would put the centers in a stabilizer direction. The normalized transforms $W_i$ converge to a nonconstant $W_*$, so there are two small disks on which $W_*$ varies with disjoint ranges, and for large $i$ every $W_i$ varies on both. For two distinct indices, (4.8) says $W_j$ takes the single value $W_j(p_i)$ on the variation set of $W_i$ in both disks. Taking a point in each disk contradicts the disjoint ranges.

5.A probability flow in the subexponential case

Now suppose the unbounded moduli grow subexponentially:

$$\limsup_{j\to\infty}M(j)^{1/j}\le1.\tag{5.1}$$

Then $u$ need not be Hölder and magnification is unavailable. Instead the proof builds a tree of small balls, each carrying an interval of values of $u$. At each node either two separated variations survive expansion, or many children carry a substantial part of the interval. A probability flow down the tree forces the first alternative, at arbitrarily deep levels.

Node intervals and transition probabilities

Fix $x_0$ where $u$ varies and a closed ball $K$ around it inside $U$. A node is a center $x\in K$, a scale $R=r_k$, and an open interval $J=(j_-,j_+)\subset(0,1)$ inside the range of $u$ on a fixed enlargement:

$$J\subset\Bigl(\min_{\overline B(x,c_0R)}u,\ \max_{\overline B(x,c_0R)}u\Bigr).\tag{5.2}$$

Write $\Delta=|J|$. Children have scale $r=\theta R$ with $\theta=a^{-D}$, for one subdivision depth $D$ used throughout. The candidates are a maximal $r$-separated set $Y$ in an annulus $(A-1)R\le d(x,\cdot)\le(B_0+1)R$, so

$$\#Y\le C_Ya^{QD}.\tag{5.3}$$

For a candidate $y$, $I_y^{\rm in}$ and $I_y^{\rm out}$ are the range intervals of $u$ on $\overline B(y,2r)$ and $\overline B(y,c_0r)$; the child interval is $J_y=J\cap\operatorname{int}I_y^{\rm out}$, with relative length $t_y=|J_y|/\Delta$, and

$$|J\cap I_y^{\rm out}|=t_y\Delta.\tag{5.4}$$

With $\kappa=c_*a^{-QD}$, a node is good if two candidates $y^-,y^+$ vary in the bottom and top quarters of $J$, with outer ranges a quarter of $J$ apart:

$$|I_{y^-}^{\rm in}\cap(j_-,j_-+\Delta/4)|\ge\kappa\Delta,\qquad|I_{y^+}^{\rm in}\cap(j_-+3\Delta/4,j_+)|\ge\kappa\Delta,\tag{5.5}$$
$$\dist\bigl(I_{y^-}^{\rm out},I_{y^+}^{\rm out}\bigr)\ge\Delta/4.\tag{5.6}$$

A good node is what §6 needs: two separated regions of variation. The exponents are fixed once:

$$b_*=\frac12,\qquad\tau=\frac1{20},\qquad\beta=\frac{\tau}{4(Q+1)},\qquad\gamma=\frac\beta2.\tag{5.7}$$

Each node gets an $Hr$-separated set of children with probabilities $p_y$ summing to one, such that

$$p_y\le a^{-\gamma D}t_y,\qquad p_y\le a^{-b_*D}t_y^2\qquad\text{at nodes that are not good},\tag{5.8}$$
$$p_y\le a^{-\gamma D}t_y,\qquad p_y\le a^{\tau D}t_y^2\qquad\text{at good nodes}.\tag{5.9}$$

The linear bounds multiply along a branch and telescope the interval ratios: a branch's probability is small compared with its interval. The quadratic bounds compare probability with squared interval length, which the measure controls. At a node that is not good the quadratic bound is much stronger, and that is the lever.

Geometric choices and many large children

The constants $c_0$, $A$, $B_0$, $c_{\rm pat}$ and the separation $H$ are fixed before $D$, so that expanded inner balls fit inside expanded outer balls with room to spare; (5.10) is the inequality that makes this possible.

Lemma 5.1 (Many children at a node that is not good). For large $D$, every node that is not good has at least $c_Ha^D$ mutually $Hr$-separated candidates with $t_y\ge1/8$, where $c_H\gt0$ is independent of the node and $D$.

Every level continuum $C_t$, $t\in J$, crosses each sphere about $x$ in the candidate annulus, so at each radius the candidates' ranges cover $J$. If two of them had small $t_y$ while meeting the bottom and top quarters, the node would be good. So at each of about $a^D$ radii some candidate has $t_y\ge1/8$, and uniform probabilities on them satisfy (5.8).

The capacity estimate

Lemma 5.2 (Separated capacity). For large $D$, uniformly over all nodes, there is an $Hr$-separated $Y'\subset Y$ with $$\sum_{y\in Y'}\min\bigl\{a^{-\gamma D}t_y,\ a^{\tau D}t_y^2\bigr\}\ge1.\tag{5.11}$$
Where subexponential growth is used

If (5.11) failed, (5.12), candidates with $t_y\ge a^{-(\tau+\gamma)D}$ would have total length at most $C_Ha^{\gamma D}$, (5.13). Test the levels at an intermediate radius $s=a^{-D'}R$, $D'=\lceil\beta D\rceil$: there are about $a^{D'}$ radial bins, more than the large candidates can occupy, so a definite measure $ca^{D'}\Delta$ of (bin, level) pairs is free of large candidates, (5.14). Each free level continuum crosses a ball of radius $s$, and expanding at scale $s$ makes that crossing macroscopic at relative scale $r/s=a^{-(D-D')}$, where weights of energy $M(D-D')$ block it, (5.15)–(5.16). Comparing energies gives $\sum_{\text{small }y}t_y^2\ge ca^{-(Q-2)D'}/M(D-D')$, (5.17). Subexponential growth gives $M(D-D')\le a^{\tau D/4}$ for large $D$, and then the small candidates alone have capacity at least $ca^{\tau D/2}$, contradicting (5.12). The node's interval length cancels, so one $D$ works for every node.

At good nodes, normalize the capacities of $Y'$ into probabilities; they satisfy (5.9). At the other nodes use Lemma 5.1's uniform rule.

Common target patterns

At a good node, expand by the node's fixed $h=v^{-1}$. The paths joining the extrema of the two inner balls land in balls of radius $c_{\rm pat}\theta/2$, and the outer balls' images contain balls of radius $10c_{\rm pat}\theta$, which is (5.18). Rounding to a fixed finite net gives an ordered pair of target balls $(\mathcal B^-,\mathcal B^+)$, the node's pattern. For $u\circ h^{-1}$, each pattern ball contains a path with endpoint variation at least $\kappa\Delta$, and the two whole-ball ranges are $\Delta/4$ apart. There are finitely many patterns, independently of depth.

The tree and its flow

Start at a root of scale $R_0$ with an interval $J_0$ of length $\Delta_0$ and build the tree. Children stay within $2(B_0+1)R$ of a parent of scale $R$, (5.19), and generation-$n$ centers are $Hr_{k_0+Dn}$-separated, so the measure enlargements in (3.6) are disjoint at each level and

$$\sum_i\Delta_i^2\le E_0,\qquad E_0=C\mu(Z)\lt\infty.\tag{5.20}$$

Let $P_i$ be the product of transitions down to node $i$, and $m_i$ the number of its ancestors that are not good. Multiplying (5.8)–(5.9) along the branch,

$$P_i\le a^{-\gamma Dn}\frac{\Delta_i}{\Delta_0},\qquad\Delta_i^2\ge\Delta_0^2P_i\,a^{D((b_*+\tau)m_i-\tau n)},\tag{5.21}$$
$$\frac{P_i}{P_j}\le a^{-\gamma Dm}\frac{\Delta_i}{\Delta_j}\le a^{-\gamma Dm}\quad\text{if $i$ is $m$ generations below $j$}.\tag{5.22}$$
Lemma 5.3 (Levels with substantial good mass). Infinitely many generations have total probability at least $1/2$ on good nodes.

By (5.21), (5.20) and Jensen's inequality,

$$\frac{E_0}{\Delta_0^2}\ \ge\ \sum_iP_i\,a^{D((b_*+\tau)m_i-\tau n)}\ \ge\ a^{D((b_*+\tau)\sum_iP_im_i-\tau n)},$$

so the average number of bad ancestors per generation is at most $\tau/(b_*+\tau)+O(1/n)=1/11+O(1/n)$. That average is the mean bad mass over the first $n$ generations. If good mass were eventually below $1/2$, bad mass would be eventually above $1/2$, far more than $1/11$. Every bad step multiplies a branch's squared interval by $a^{b_*D}$ more than its probability allows, and the energy budget $E_0$ cannot pay for many of them. Figure 7 runs the budget.

Proposition 5.4 (Properties of the flow). The construction gives a fixed $D$ and a tree with $Hr_{k_0+Dn}$-separated generation-$n$ centers and descendant displacement (5.19); nested nonempty value intervals; probabilities satisfying (5.21) and (5.22); infinitely many levels with good mass at least $1/2$; a fixed expansion at every node; and at each good node a pattern from a finite list, whose two target balls contain paths of endpoint variation at least $\kappa\Delta_i$, have whole-ball ranges $\Delta_i/4$ apart, and lie in the images of the outer balls.

6.Same-depth expansions and the measure comparison

Take a generation with good mass at least $1/2$. Its good nodes have expansions with the same pattern but different punctures. Comparing them two at a time, Lemma 3.8 forces one node of each pair to spend oscillation of $u$ near the other's puncture. Pulled back, those neighbourhoods carry more $\mu$-mass in total than their overlaps allow.

Proposition 6.1. The moduli $M(n)$ cannot be unbounded while satisfying $\limsup_nM(n)^{1/n}\le1$.

At generation $n$ write $k=k_0+Dn$, $R=a^{-k}$ and $p_i=h_iz_0$ for the punctures. The flow gives

$$P_i\le a^{-\gamma Dn}\frac{\Delta_i}{\Delta_0},\qquad\frac{P_j}{P_A}\le a^{-\gamma Dm}\quad\text{if $j$ lies $m$ generations below $A$}.\tag{6.1}$$

A probability class and separated punctures

Lemma 6.2 (Bounded puncture multiplicity). For every fixed $C\gt0$, the balls $\overline B(p_i,CR)$ over the nodes $i$ of generation $n$ have uniformly bounded multiplicity.

Discarding nodes of tiny probability and sorting the rest by pattern and dyadic probability class leaves a class $\mathcal I$ of $\cN$ nodes with comparable probabilities $P_*\le P_i\le2P_*$ and

$$\sum_{i\in\mathcal I}P_i\ge\frac ck,\tag{6.2}$$
$$\cN P_*\ge\frac c{2k}.\tag{6.3}$$

Since $P_i\le a^{-\gamma Dn}$, the class is large: $\cN\ge ck^{-1}a^{\gamma Dn}$. Punctures are compared at the scale $a^{-l}$, with $\zeta=\gamma/Q$, $m=\lfloor\zeta n\rfloor$ and $l=Dm$, so that $Ql\le\gamma Dn$.

Lemma 6.3 (Probability near a puncture). For every generation-$n$ node $i$, $$\sum_{j:\ d(p_j,p_i)\le a^{-l}}P_j\le Ca^{-\gamma Dm}.\tag{6.4}$$

Split a geodesic word for $v_j^{-1}$ as $w_ju_j'$ with $|w_j|=l$. A nearby puncture pins down $w_j$, by (2.4), up to boundedly many choices; and reading the same word backwards, the descendant's position under a fixed ancestor $A$ pins down $u_j'$. So each ancestor has only boundedly many descendants near $p_i$, each with probability at most $P_Aa^{-\gamma Dm}$ by (6.1). Consequently all but a vanishing fraction of the class are far from each $p_i$, and at least $c\cN^2$ unordered pairs are far pairs:

$$d_{ij}:=d(p_i,p_j)\gt a^{-l}.\tag{6.5}$$

For far pairs, $R/d_{ij}\le a^{-(k-l)}\to0$.

Comparison on a compact common domain

The puncture estimate (2.3) gives a radius $A_hR$ beyond which preimages stay in a compact part of $U$; this is (6.6). Put $E_i=\overline B(p_i,A_hR)$. For far pairs at large generations, $E_i$ and $E_j$ are disjoint, each lies compactly in the other's transformed domain, and the pattern balls avoid both.

Lemma 6.4 (Pair alternative). Every far pair at a sufficiently large generation has an endpoint $i$ with $$\osc_{h_i^{-1}E_j}u\ge\frac{\kappa}{16}\Delta_i.\tag{6.7}$$

Write $F_i=u\circ h_i^{-1}$. If $F_j$ already oscillates on $E_i$, endpoint $j$ qualifies. Otherwise one of the two shared pattern balls has an $F_j$-range far from its range on $E_i$. In that ball, node $i$'s variation path supplies level continua of $F_i$; if $F_i$ were nearly constant on $E_j$, these continua would avoid $E_j$, reach $E_i$, and carry an oscillation of $F_j$ at least $\Delta_j/32$. Lemma 3.8, applied to the approximants with the pair held fixed, contradicts the vanishing energies of Lemma 3.5. So $F_i$ oscillates on $E_j$, which is (6.7).

Cost balls and total multiplicity

For an ordered far pair put $z_{ij}=h_i^{-1}p_j$. Points of $h_i^{-1}E_j$ are close to $x_i$, and the two-sided puncture bound gives

$$D_{z_{ij}}\asymp R/d_{ij}.\tag{6.8}$$

The lower expansion bound then puts the preimage in a ball of radius $s_{ij}=C_2R^2/d_{ij}^2$:

$$h_i^{-1}E_j\subseteq\overline B(z_{ij},s_{ij}).\tag{6.9}$$

Enlarge it to $\mathcal D_{ij}=\overline B(z_{ij},C_\mu s_{ij})$, as (3.6) requires. If $i$ is the endpoint chosen by Lemma 6.4, then

$$\mu(\mathcal D_{ij})\ge c\kappa^2\Delta_i^2.\tag{6.10}$$

The radius is $O(R/d_{ij})$ times the denominator, a vanishing fraction, so the enlargement keeps a small image:

$$h_i\mathcal D_{ij}\subseteq\overline B(p_j,C_3R).\tag{6.11}$$

This separation of scales, $R^2/d_{ij}^2$ against $R/d_{ij}$, is what the paper's second figure shows; Figure 8 computes it with exact Möbius maps.

For fixed $i$, (6.11) and Lemma 6.2 bound how many partners $j$ can charge a given point. And since $d(x_i,z_{ij})\le CR/d_{ij}$,

$$\mathcal D_{ij}\subseteq\overline B(x_i,C_4Ra^l),\tag{6.12}$$

and the $HR$-separation of the centers bounds by $Ca^{Ql}$ how many indices $i$ can charge a given point.

Conclusion of the proof of Proposition 6.1

Assign each far pair to an endpoint that satisfies Lemma 6.4. The multiplicity bounds give

$$\sum_{\{i,j\}\text{ far, assigned to }i}\mu(\mathcal D_{ij})\le Ca^{Ql}\mu(Z).\tag{6.13}$$

On the other side, each paying endpoint has $\Delta_i\ge\Delta_0a^{\gamma Dn}P_*$ by (6.1), and there are at least $c\cN^2$ far pairs, so by (6.10) and (6.3)

$$c\cN^2\kappa^2\Delta_0^2a^{2\gamma Dn}P_*^2\ \ge\ c'k^{-2}a^{2\gamma Dn}.\tag{6.14}$$

Since $Ql\le\gamma Dn$, comparing the two gives $a^{\gamma Dn}\le Ck^2$, impossible as $k=k_0+Dn\to\infty$.

7.Completion of the proof

If $M(n)$ were unbounded, the record selection of §3 would give either exponential records, excluded by Proposition 4.5, or subexponential growth, excluded by Proposition 6.1. So $\sup_nM(n)\lt\infty$. Theorem 2.5 uniformizes the boundary, and Proposition 2.6 supplies the proper cocompact action with finite kernel. This proves Theorem 1.1.

8.Consequences

Corollary 8.1. If $G$ is a torsion-free hyperbolic group and $\partial G$ is homeomorphic to $\Sphere$, then $G$ is the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic three-manifold.

Point stabilizers of a proper action are finite, hence trivial, so the action is free and $\HH^3/G$ is a closed manifold, possibly nonorientable.

Corollary 8.2 (Virtual structure in the torsion-free case). Let $G$ be torsion-free hyperbolic with $\partial G$ homeomorphic to $\Sphere$. Some finite-index subgroup of $G$ is isomorphic to $\pi_1(\Sigma)\rtimes\ZZ$ for a closed connected orientable surface $\Sigma$. In particular $G$ has positive virtual first Betti number. Moreover $G$ is virtually compact special and residually finite.

These follow from Corollary 8.1 by Agol's virtual fibering and virtual specialness theorems and Bergeron–Wise's cubulation, applied to the orientation cover.
