Catalan's Constant, by Determinants

How a family of $48N\times48N$ determinants, which would be rational if $G$ were, gets caught between an arithmetic floor and an analytic ceiling about $10^{-4}$ apart. The page follows the September 2026 proof section by section.

Status (October 7, 2026)

The paper Catalan's constant is irrational (OpenAI, dated September 24, 2026) was posted on October 6, 2026 in the openai/math repository (commit adc7f12), as family 005 of that collection. It has not been refereed.

The repository also contains a Lean 4 development, OAI/NumberTheory/Catalan (981 files, about 574,000 lines). Its top-level theorem has the statement of the comparator challenge Catalan.lean, namely Irrational (∑' j : ℕ, (-1)^j / (2j+1)^2). The challenge file is the statement alone, ending in sorry, as comparator challenges do. Its configuration names the solution module OAI.NumberTheory.Catalan.Main and permits only the axioms propext, Quot.sound and Classical.choice. A text search of the solution module on October 7, 2026 finds no sorry and no native_decide. This page has not compiled the development or run the comparator.

The paper notes that Sun has announced a proof of the same statement (arXiv:2609.04176), and uses nothing from it.

This page explains the argument as the paper gives it. Section, proposition and equation numbers match the paper. The figures are models. Several recompute a numbered step exactly at the smallest size $N=1$, but they are not part of the proof.

1The theorem

Catalan's constant is the alternating sum of inverse odd squares,

$$G=\beta(2)=\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{(-1)^j}{(2j+1)^2}=1-\frac19+\frac1{25}-\frac1{49}+\cdots=0.9159655941\ldots$$

Here $\beta(s)=\sum_j(-1)^j(2j+1)^{-s}$ is the Dirichlet beta function, so $G=L(2,\chi_{-4})$ for the odd character of conductor $4$. The odd values of $\beta$ are rational multiples of powers of $\pi$, for example $\beta(1)=\pi/4$ and $\beta(3)=\pi^3/32$. The even values have resisted every method so far, and $G$ is the first of them.

Theorem 1.1. Catalan's constant is irrational.

Earlier work. Before this paper, the arithmetic of $G$ was known only through families of values and through approximations that fell just short.

  • Families. Rivoal and Zudilin (2003) proved that infinitely many even beta values are irrational, and that one of $\beta(2),\beta(4),\ldots,\beta(14)$ is. Zudilin (2019) and Lai–Zhou (2022) shortened the list to $\beta(2),\ldots,\beta(10)$. Fischler (2020) proved quantitative versions for Dirichlet $L$-values. None of these singles out $\beta(2)$.
  • Approximations. To prove a single number irrational, one needs nonzero integer linear forms $A_mG-B_m$ that tend to $0$. Apéry's recurrences (1979) and Beukers' integrals (1979) achieve this for $\zeta(2)$ and $\zeta(3)$. For $G$, Zudilin (2003) built Apéry-like recurrences, a continued fraction and double integrals. The quotients $B_m/A_m$ converge fast, but once the denominators are cleared the linear forms no longer tend to zero. Work of Rivoal, Krattenthaler–Rivoal, Krattenthaler–Zudilin, Nesterenko, Marcovecchio–Viola and Eskandari sharpened the denominators and the approximation exponents. The denominator cost was still too high.
  • Neighbors. Calegari, Dimitrov and Tang (2024) proved $1$, $\pi^2$ and $L(2,\chi_{-3})$ linearly independent over $\Q$. Their character has conductor $3$, not $4$. Calegari (2005) proved a $2$-adic analogue of $G$ irrational. The corresponding real identity carries an extra $\pi^2$ term, which is why the $2$-adic result does not settle the real one. The same extra $\zeta(2)$ has to be cancelled here (§2.1).

The strategy. Instead of one sequence of linear forms, the paper builds determinants $\Delta_N$ of size $n=48N$. Each entry is a combination of moments of two elementary kernels, and every moment is a rational combination of $1$, $G$ and $\zeta(2)$. The rows are designed so that $\zeta(2)$ cancels from every entry. So if $G$ were rational, every $\Delta_N$ would be rational. A nonzero rational number $\Delta$ obeys the product formula

$$\log|\Delta|=\sum_p v_p(\Delta)\log p .$$

Bounding the denominators of $\Delta_N$ prime by prime therefore bounds $\log|\Delta_N|$ from below. An integral formula for the same determinant bounds it from above. Both bounds live on the scale $n^2$, and the paper tracks the normalized logarithm

$$\cL_N=\frac{\log|\Delta_N|}{(48N)^2}-\frac12\log 2. \tag{1}$$

Assuming $G\in\Q$, the finite places give $\liminf\cL_N>-2.29084$ along a sequence of prime $N$ for which $\Delta_N\neq0$. Without any assumption, the real place gives $\limsup\cL_N\le-2.290939875$. The stated constants are $10^{-4}$ apart, and the exact lower constant, $-2.290810\ldots$, leaves a true margin of $1.3\times10^{-4}$. Every constant in the $n^2$ terms has to be computed exactly, which is why much of the paper is bookkeeping and two of its steps are finite certificates.

Zudilin's determinantal criterion (2017) is the closest precedent. There, Hankel determinants of a positive moment sequence are squared-Vandermonde integrals, so positivity gives nonvanishing for free. Zudilin also explained why the denominators of the then-known Catalan families were too large for his criterion. Here the determinant is mixed and signed, so nonvanishing (§4) and the real estimate (§5–7) need new arguments. The paper identifies three features that make the comparison close:

  1. Chebyshev rows give both the Taylor contact that cancels $\zeta(2)$ and the divisibility needed at primes. Denominators of order $p^2$ and of order $p$ are controlled in two separate layers.
  2. Nonvanishing is arithmetic. When $N=p$ is prime, Frobenius folds the $48p\times48p$ matrix modulo $p$ into three fixed $48\times48$ rational matrices. One finite computation shows they are invertible.
  3. The real estimate respects two sheets of the parametrization $t=2x/(1+x^2)$. A bounded holomorphic interpolant handles one range of configurations, Hadamard's inequality the other. The remaining logarithmic energy is reduced to one-variable functions, and explicit rational trial coefficients finish the bound.

2Polynomial rows and mixed moments

Everything is built from a single scale $N$. The size, degrees and padding are fixed multiples of it:

$$n=48N,\quad a=11N,\quad b=7N,\quad q=g=4N,\quad h=2N,\qquad L=59N,\quad C=63N,\quad H=65N,\quad A=19N. \tag{2}$$

These ratios are where all the constants of the paper come from. For instance $L=n+a$ is the contact order, $H$ bounds the degrees, and $A$ is the lowest degree in any row.

Two sheets of one variable. Put $f(t)=\sqrt{1-t^2}$ and $w=t/(1+f)$. Then $t=2w/(1+w^2)$ and $f=(1-w^2)/(1+w^2)$: this is the rational parametrization of the circle $t^2+f^2=1$. The involution $w\mapsto w^{-1}$, written as a star, fixes $t$ and changes the sign of $f$. The rows are

$$R_r=(1-t)^h\,t^{C-1}\,w^{r-g},\qquad P_r=\frac{R_r+R_r^*}{2},\qquad D_r=\frac{t\,(R_r^*-R_r)}{2f}\qquad(0\le r\lt n). \tag{3}$$

Since $(w+w^{-1})/2=1/t$ and $(w^{-1}-w)/2=f/t$, these are Chebyshev polynomials in disguise. With $d=|r-g|$,

$$P_r=(1-t)^h t^{C-1}T_d(1/t),\qquad D_r=\sgn(r-g)\,(1-t)^h t^{C-1}U_{d-1}(1/t).$$

Both are polynomials with integer coefficients, supported in degrees $A,\ldots,H-1$ (4). Their key property is Taylor contact. Since $w=t/2+O(t^3)$,

$$\frac{tP_r}{f}-D_r=\frac{tR_r}{f}=O\big(t^{C+r-g}\big)=O(t^L). \tag{5}$$

Moments. Two kernels are integrated against the rows. For $i,j\ge0$,

$$M(i,j)=\int_{-1}^1\!\int_0^1\frac{|t|}{f(t)}\,\frac{t^is^j}{1-ts}\,ds\,dt,\qquad Z(i,j)=\int_0^1\!\int_0^1\frac{t^is^j}{1-ts}\,ds\,dt, \tag{6}$$

extended bilinearly to polynomials. The raw entries are $F_j(r)=M(P_r,j)-\tfrac32Z(D_r,j)$ for $b\le j\lt L$, and the determinant is

$$\Delta_N=\det_{0\le r,k\lt n}\Big(M\big(P_r,s^{b+k}(1-s)^q\big)-\tfrac32 Z\big(D_r,s^{b+k}(1-s)^q\big)\Big). \tag{7}$$

Expanding $(1-s)^q$ shows that this matrix is $\cF\cT$. Here $\cF$ is the $n\times(n+q)$ matrix of raw columns $F_j$, and $\cT$ is an integer "filter" matrix of signed binomial coefficients (8). The filter is the reason the column pool has $q$ more columns than the determinant needs. Every raw column it uses lies in the contact range $j\lt L$.

2.1Moment evaluation and the cancellation of ζ(2)

With $c_l=4^{-l}\binom{2l}{l}$, the one-variable moments are $m_i=\int t^i|t|\,dt/f=2/((i+1)c_{i/2})$ for even $i$ and $0$ for odd $i$ (9). Expanding $1/(1-ts)$ gives $M(i,j)=\sum_{u\ge0}m_{i+u}/(j+u+1)$ (10). So $M$ is determined by a diagonal recurrence $M(i,j)-M(i+1,j+1)=m_i/(j+1)$ and its values on the two boundaries $M(d,0)$ and $M(0,d)$. Each boundary satisfies a three-term recurrence (11) that steps $d$ by $2$. The recurrence forces $M(1,0)=2$, which leaves two free starting values. They are the only transcendental input:

$$M(0,0)=4G,\qquad M(0,1)=\frac{\pi^2}{4}=\frac32\zeta(2). \tag{12}$$

The first comes from $t=\sin\theta$ and $-4\int_0^{\pi/4}\log\tan v\,dv=4G$. The second is the value at $1$ of the series of $(\arcsin x)^2$. Propagating the starts through the recurrences gives

$$M(i,j)=M^0(i,j)+4G\,c_{(i-j)/2}+\tfrac32\zeta(2)\,c_{(j-i-1)/2},\qquad Z(i,j)=Z^0(i,j)+[i=j]\,\zeta(2), \tag{17}$$

where $M^0,Z^0$ are explicit rational arrays, built from harmonic sums and the central binomials $c_l$ (14)–(16), and $c_z=0$ unless $z$ is a nonnegative integer. In words, $G$ sits on the main diagonal and every second diagonal below it ($i-j\ge0$ even), and $\zeta(2)$ on every second diagonal above it ($j-i$ odd).

Proposition 2.1. Every raw entry $F_j(r)$ lies in $\Q+\Q G$. Consequently, if $G\in\Q$, then $\Delta_N\in\Q$ for every $N\ge1$.
Proof

Since $1/f=\sum_l c_lt^{2l}$, the $\zeta(2)$-coefficient of $F_j(r)$ is

$$\tfrac32\Big(\sum_i[t^i]P_r\,c_{(j-i-1)/2}-[t^j]D_r\Big)=\tfrac32\,[t^j]\Big(\frac{tP_r}{f}-D_r\Big),$$

which vanishes for $j\lt L$ by the contact (5). What remains is a rational combination of $1$ and $G$:

$$F_j(r)=\sum_i[t^i]P_r\,\big(M^0(i,j)+4G\,c_{(i-j)/2}\big)-\tfrac32\sum_i[t^i]D_r\,Z^0(i,j). \tag{20}$$

This is where the weight $\tfrac32$ in (7) comes from. It is the same $\tfrac32$ that appears in $M(0,1)=\tfrac32\zeta(2)$, and it lets the $Z$-kernel's diagonal $\zeta(2)$ cancel the $M$-kernel's off-diagonal one. Figure 1 computes these coefficients exactly.

2.2A uniform estimate at the prime two

From here to the end of §4, assume $G\in\Q$. The prime $2$ is special because the Chebyshev coefficients are highly divisible by $2$ and the moments $m_u$ are very small $2$-adically.

Proposition 2.2. With $\delta=(q+g+h)/n=5/24$, for every $N\ge1$, $$v_2(\Delta_N)\ \ge\ -\Big(\frac\delta2+\frac{\delta^2}8\Big)n^2-O_G\big(n\log(n+2)\big)=-\frac{505}{4608}\,n^2-O_G\big(n\log(n+2)\big). \tag{21}$$ The same bound holds for every full minor of the raw column matrix.
How the quadratic arises

Because $v_2(m_u)\ge u+1-\log_2(u+1)$, the series (10) converges $2$-adically and defines a "smoothed" array $M_{\rm s}$. It satisfies the same recurrences as the rational part, so it differs from $M^0+4Gc$ by only two fixed $2$-adic constants $e_1,e_2$ (24). Each raw column then splits into three types (25): smoothed columns, which are divisible by about $2^C$, and two kinds of exceptional columns. The exceptional columns of each kind are combinations of fixed coefficient vectors $\mathbf p_u$ or $\mathbf d_u$, whose valuations grow with $u$ (27).

The key observation is alternation. In a determinant, two exceptional columns that use the same $\mathbf p_u$ give zero. So $m$ columns of the first kind must use distinct $u$'s, and $\sum u\ge m(m-1)/2$. The valuation gained grows quadratically in $m$. Minimizing $-(H-b)m+\tfrac32m^2+\tfrac12l^2+C(n-m-l)$ (30) over the numbers $m,l$ of exceptional columns gives the minimum $-(\delta/2+\delta^2/8)n^2$, at $l=n-m$, $m=(2+\delta)n/4$.

3Odd primes and the finite-place lower bound

Fix an odd prime $p$ with $2\sqrt H\lt p\le H$. Primes below $2\sqrt H$ cost only $o(n^2)$, and primes above $H$ never divide a denominator. The section shows that every raw column lies in $p^{-2}\Z_p^n$, then makes invertible column operations over $\Z_p$ that push many columns into $p^{-1}\Z_p^n$ or $\Z_p^n$. If a full minor uses $r$ columns of the first kind and $s$ of the second, its valuation is at least $-(2r+s)$. Cauchy–Binet transfers this from the raw pool to the filtered determinant, because the column changes and the filter are integral. There are two layers: first residues after multiplying by $p^2$, then (for $p\gt H/2$) residues after multiplying by $p$.

3.1Digit reduction of the rational moments

Put $E(t)=(1-t^2)^{(p-1)/2}=\sum_dE_dt^d$ in $\F_p[t]$. Its even coefficients are the central binomials, $E_{2k}\equiv c_k\pmod p$, because $(-1)^k\binom{(p-1)/2}{k}\equiv4^{-k}\binom{2k}{k}$.

Lemma 3.1 (Digit reductions). For $0\le i,j\lt H$ let $\ell=j\bmod p$, $d=(j-i-1)\bmod p$, $j'=\lfloor j/p\rfloor$ and $i'=(i+1+d-\ell)/p-1$. Then $i'\ge-1$ and, with $M^0(-1,j')=k^+_{j'+1}$, $$p^2M^0(i,j)\equiv E_d\,M^0(i',j')\pmod p,\qquad p^2Z^0(i,j)\equiv\begin{cases}Z^0(\lfloor i/p\rfloor,j'),&i\equiv j\ (p),\\0,&i\not\equiv j\ (p).\end{cases} \tag{31}$$ Both sides are $p$-integral.

The lemma says that, after multiplying by $p^2$, a moment array of size $H$ looks modulo $p$ like a tiny array indexed by the high base-$p$ digits $(i',j')$, times one factor $E_d$ that depends only on the low digits. The proof is careful carry-counting. Kummer's theorem gives the valuations of the factors $\binom{z}{z/2}$ in the explicit formulas (19), and a block identity $\sum_{j\le(p-1)/2}c_j^2\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p$ comes from $(1+x)^s(1+x)^s$. The condition $p\gt2\sqrt H$ guarantees there are only two digits. Figure 2 checks (31) cell by cell.

3.2The leading layer and paired raw columns

Collect Lemma 3.1 over a whole column. For a residue $\ell$, extract coefficient vectors of the rows,

$$P'_u=[t^{(u+1)p+\ell}]\,tP(t)E(t),\qquad D'_u=[t^{up+\ell}]\,D(t),$$

so that

$$X_{kp+\ell}:=p^2F_{kp+\ell}\bmod p=\sum_uP'_uM^0(u,k)-\tfrac32\sum_uD'_uZ^0(u,k). \tag{36}$$

The $G$ term disappears: its coefficients $c_l$ are $p$-integral. If $\ell\ge H-2p$, only $u\le1$ occurs, and a small table of $M^0$ and $Z^0$ values gives

$$X_\ell=V_\ell,\qquad X_{p+\ell}=U_\ell-V_\ell,\qquad V_\ell=2P'_1-\tfrac32D'_1,\ \ U_\ell=2P'_{-1}-\tfrac32D'_0. \tag{37}$$

For $\ell\le A$ the rows have no terms of low degree, so $U_\ell=0$. Then the leading residues of $F_\ell$ and $F_{p+\ell}$ cancel, and the sum $F_\ell+F_{p+\ell}$ lies in $p^{-1}\Z_p^n$. Replacing the high column of each such pair by the pair sum is a unimodular column operation. If there are $d_0$ such pairs, any choice of $n$ columns from the pool of $n+q$ contains at least $(d_0-q)_+$ improved ones, so for $2\sqrt H\lt p\le H/2$

$$v_p(\Delta_N)\ \ge\ -2n+(d_0-q)_+. \tag{38}$$

The $q$ extra raw columns are a cost: a minor can avoid up to $q$ improved columns.

3.3The central layer and integral column elimination

For $p\gt H/2$, the large raw indices $j$ have only one digit, and these "central" columns already have denominator at most $p$. Their next residues are made of the same vectors $V_\ell$.

Lemma 3.2 (Central layer). Suppose $H/2\lt p\le H$ and write $K=L-p$, $J=H-p$. Every central column, meaning $b\le j\lt\min(p,L)$ and $j\ge J$, satisfies $$pF_j\in\Z_p^n,\qquad pF_j\equiv-\sum_{0\le\ell\lt J}\frac{V_\ell}{j-\ell}\pmod p, \tag{39}$$ and all denominators $j-\ell$ are units.
Lemma 3.3 (Elimination over the local integers). For $H/2\lt p\le H$ put $$R=K_++(K-A)_++(J-\max(b,K))_+,\qquad S=(\min(A,K)-b)_++(\min(b,J)-\max(0,K))_+.$$ Then every full raw minor, and hence $\Delta_N$, satisfies $v_p(\Delta_N)\ge-\min(2n,\,n+R,\,2R+S)$. (40)
Proof idea

Keep every noncentral column and pair up those that can be paired. The kept columns supply, as leading residues, every $V_\ell$ with $0\le\ell\lt J$ except those in an exceptional set $\cE$ of size $e$. Add $p$ times suitable integral multiples of these kept columns to each central column. This is a shear with integral inverse, and by (39) it cancels every $V_\ell$ with $\ell\notin\cE$ from the scaled residues. The scaled residues of the central columns then lie in the span of the $e$ exceptional vectors, so they have rank $d\le e$. A change of basis in $\GL_c(\Z_p)$ makes all but $d$ central columns integral. The pool now has $R$ columns with denominator $p^2$, at most $S$ with denominator $p$, and the rest integral, and a minor with $r$ and $s$ columns of the first two kinds loses at most $2r+s$.

The paper insists on two points. No column is discarded, and no column's denominator is lowered just because its leading residue depends linearly on others. Only explicit integral operations are used.

Proposition 3.4 (Finite-place bound). Assume $G\in\Q$. Along any sequence $N\to\infty$ with $\Delta_N\neq0$, $$\liminf_{N\to\infty}\Big(\frac{\log|\Delta_N|}{n^2}-\frac12\log2\Big)\ \ge\ -\frac{8609}{4608}-\Big(\frac12+\frac{505}{4608}\Big)\log2\ \gt\ -2.29084. \tag{42}$$

The first term is $\int d/2304$, from the odd primes, since $n^2=2304N^2$. The second is the prime $2$ (Prop. 2.2) plus the normalization in (1). The primes below $2\sqrt H$ contribute $O(n\sqrt H\log H)=o(n^2)$, and primes above $H$ contribute nothing. The final digits use a rational upper bound $\log2\lt0.693149$, obtained from the series $\log2=2\sum_j3^{-(2j+1)}/(2j+1)$.

4Nonvanishing along the prime sequence

The product formula needs $\Delta_N\neq0$. For Hankel determinants of a positive measure this is automatic, but $\Delta_N$ is a mixed, signed determinant, and the paper does not claim it has a sign. Instead it proves nonvanishing arithmetically, by computing $\Delta_N$ exactly modulo $p$ when $N=p$ is itself prime.

Proposition 4.1. Assume $G\in\Q$. For every sufficiently large prime $p$, the determinant with $N=p$ satisfies $$v_p(\Delta_p)=-96p. \tag{46}$$ In particular $\Delta_p\neq0$.

Since $2n=96p$, this says the leading layer is as large as it can be: $p^{2n}\Delta_p$ is a $p$-adic unit. The proof reduces the $48p\times48p$ matrix modulo $p$ to three fixed $48\times48$ rational matrices. Build the base rows $\underline P_r,\underline D_r$ with $N=1$, including one auxiliary row $r=48$, and set

$$\cB(r,k)=\sum_{j=0}^4(-1)^j\binom4j\Big\{M^0(\underline P_r,7+k+j)-\tfrac32Z^0(\underline D_r,7+k+j)\Big\}\qquad(0\le r\le48,\ 0\le k\lt48). \tag{47}$$

With $\cB_0$ the first $48$ rows and $\cB_1$ the last $48$, the finite certificate proves

$$\det\cB_0\neq0,\qquad\det(\cB_0+\cB_1)\neq0,\qquad\det(\cB_0-\cB_1)\neq0. \tag{48}$$

4.1Two palindromic bases

Over $\F_p$, the palindromic polynomials of degree at most $2p-2$ form a $p$-dimensional space $\cP_p$. It has two natural bases:

$$U_\ell(w)=(2w)^\ell(1+w^2)^{p-1-\ell},\qquad Q_i(w)=w^i\sum_{j=0}^{p-i-1}w^{2j}\qquad(0\le\ell,i\lt p).$$

The change of basis $Q_i=\sum_\ell a_{\ell i}U_\ell$ is lower triangular with $a_{ii}=2^{-i}$, so $\det\mathbf a=2^{-p(p-1)/2}\neq0$ (49). Let $\mathbf b=\mathbf a\Pi$, where $\Pi e_0=0$ and $\Pi e_i=e_{p-i}$ (50). Because $\binom{p-1}{j}\equiv(-1)^j$, one has $Q_i+w^pQ_{p-i}=w^i(1-w^2)^{p-1}$ for $1\le i\lt p$, and $Q_0=(1-w^2)^{p-1}$. Dividing by $(1+w^2)^{p-1}$ gives, with $t=2w/(1+w^2)$,

$$E(t)\,w^i=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}t^\ell\big(a_{\ell i}+b_{\ell i}w^p\big). \tag{51}$$

4.2Frobenius and the two extraction shifts

Now take $N=p$ and write each row index as $r=pr_0+i$ with $0\le i\lt p$. In characteristic $p$, Frobenius maps $t,w,f$ to $t'=t^p$, $w'=w^p$, $f'=f^p$, and these satisfy the same relations. Since $C=63p$, $h=2p$ and $g=4p$, the row at scale $p$ factors through a row at scale $1$:

$$tR_rE(t)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}t^\ell\,t'\Big(a_{\ell i}\,\underline R_{r_0}(t',w')+b_{\ell i}\,\underline R_{r_0+1}(t',w')\Big). \tag{52}$$

The factor $w'$ in the second term moves to the next base row, which is why the auxiliary row $48$ is needed. Separating the parts fixed and negated by the star gives separate identities for $tP_rE$ and for $D_r$ (53)–(54). Extracting the coefficient of $t^\ell$ identifies the vectors $P'_u,D'_u$ of §3.2 with the base rows (55). The factor $t'$ in (52) is exactly absorbed by the shift $u+1$ in the $P$-extraction.

4.3The residue blocks and their determinant

Order the rows by $(i,r_0)$ and the columns by $(\ell,k_0)$, where $k=\ell+pk_0$. Then the filter respects residues, since $s^{7p+k}(1-s)^{4p}=s^\ell(s^p)^{7+k_0}(1-s^p)^4$ in $\F_p[s]$. Combining this with (36) and (55), the matrix $p^2\cF_p\cT$ reduces modulo $p$, after reordering, to

$$\cL_p=\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\otimes\cB_0+\mathbf b^{\mathsf T}\otimes\cB_1,\qquad \cL_p\big((\mathbf a^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\otimes I_{48}\big)=I_p\otimes\cB_0+\Pi^{\mathsf T}\otimes\cB_1. \tag{59}$$

The permutation-like matrix $\Pi$ has eigenvalue $0$ once (on $e_0$) and $\pm1$ each $(p-1)/2$ times (on $e_i\pm e_{p-i}$). Diagonalizing it splits the determinant:

$$\det\cL_p=(\det\mathbf a)^{48}\,\det\cB_0\,\det(\cB_0+\cB_1)^{(p-1)/2}\det(\cB_0-\cB_1)^{(p-1)/2}. \tag{60}$$

4.4An exact certificate for the fixed matrices

Every entry of $\cB$ is built from the recurrences for $m_i$, $k^\pm_d$, the diagonal reduction, harmonic sums and the Chebyshev recurrence. Every denominator is a product of integers of size at most $65$, so the whole construction can be carried out in $\F_{101}$. Gaussian elimination there finds $3\times48$ nonzero pivots, with only two row swaps, $(30,31)$ and $(45,46)$, for $\sigma=1$. This is Table 1, and it proves (48).

Completing the proof of Proposition 4.1. Exclude the finitely many primes dividing the denominator of $G$, a denominator of an entry of $\cB$, or a numerator or denominator of the three nonzero determinants in (48). For any other prime $p\gt260$, all three matrices are invertible modulo $p$, so $\det\cL_p\neq0$ by (60). Hence $p^{2n}\Delta_p=\det(p^2\cF_p\cT)$ is a unit in $\Z_{(p)}$, and $v_p(\Delta_p)=-2n=-96p$. The modulus $101$ only certifies three fixed matrices; the growing determinants use arbitrary large primes.

5The real-place determinant estimates

Now the upper bound. This part makes no rationality assumption, and every constant must be uniform in the positions of $2n$ integration variables, including when they nearly collide. Write $\alpha=a/n=11/48$, $\beta=b/n=7/48$, $\gamma=g/n=q/n=4/48$, $\eta=h/n=2/48$ and $D_*=n-1-2g=40N-1$ (61).

5.1The integral and its two sheets

With $d\mu(t)=|t|\,dt/f(t)$, $A_r(t)=P_r(t)-\tfrac32\mathbf 1_{t\gt0}\,\frac{f(t)}tD_r(t)$ and $\psi_k(s)=s^{b+k}(1-s)^q$, each entry of (7) is $\iint A_r(t)\,\psi_k(s)\,(1-ts)^{-1}\,ds\,d\mu(t)$. Two classical identities turn the determinant into one big integral:

  • Andréief's identity, used twice, turns the determinant of single integrals into
$$\Delta_N=\frac1{(n!)^2}\int_{(-1,1)^n}\int_{(0,1)^n}\det[A_r(t_i)]\,\det\Big[\frac1{1-t_is_j}\Big]\,\det[\psi_k(s_j)]\,ds\,d\mu^n(t). \tag{62}$$
  • Cauchy's double alternant evaluates the middle factor (63), and the last factor is $V(s)\prod_js_j^b(1-s_j)^q$:
$$\det\Big[\frac1{1-t_is_j}\Big]=\frac{V(t)\,V(s)}{\prod_{i,j}(1-t_is_j)}. \tag{63}$$

Use the coordinate $x=t/(1+\sqrt{1-t^2})$, so $t=2x/(1+x^2)$ (64), with $x\in(-1,1)$. Since $fD_r/t=(R_r^*-R_r)/2$, the row is $(R_r+R_r^*)/2$ on $t\lt0$ and $(5R_r-R_r^*)/4$ on $t\gt0$. Here $R_r\propto x^{r-g}$ is evaluated on the near sheet $x$, and $R_r^*\propto x^{g-r}$ on the far sheet $1/x$. Factoring out the common powers leaves the sheet determinant

$$\cR_n(x)=\det\big[\xi_{\rm f}(x_i)\,x_i^{g-r}+\xi_{\rm n}(x_i)\,x_i^{r-g}\big]_{r,i},\qquad(\xi_{\rm f},\xi_{\rm n})=\begin{cases}(\tfrac12,\tfrac12),&x\lt0,\\(-\tfrac14,\tfrac54),&x\gt0,\end{cases}$$

and an exact formula (65) with one $t$-Vandermonde and two $s$-Vandermondes. Everything except $\cR_n$ is positive. The analytic problem is to bound $\cR_n$.

5.2A uniform interpolating function

Factor out the far-sheet weight: the $i$th column becomes $\xi_{\rm f}(x_i)x_i^{g-(n-1)}\big(x_i^{n-1-r}+c_ix_i^{D_*}x_i^r\big)$, with $c_i=\xi_{\rm n}/\xi_{\rm f}$ equal to $1$ or $-5$ (66). If one holomorphic function $h_*$ has the values $h_*(x_i)=c_ix_i^{D_*}$, the bracket is $x_i^{n-1-r}+h_*(x_i)x_i^r$. The first estimate applies when the nodes are not too concentrated near $0$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{1-x_i^2}{1+x_i^2}\ \le\ D_*. \tag{67}$$

Since $(1-x^2)/(1+x^2)=f(t)=\sqrt{1-t^2}$, this condition says the average of $\sqrt{1-t_i^2}$ is at most about $5/6$.

Lemma 5.1. Let $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ be distinct nonzero reals in $(-1,1)$ satisfying (67). There is $h_*$, holomorphic near the closed unit disk, with $$h_*(x_i)=c_ix_i^{D_*},\qquad c_i=\begin{cases}1,&x_i\lt0,\\-5,&x_i\gt0,\end{cases}\qquad\sup_{|z|\le1}|h_*(z)|\le K_0n,\quad K_0=10e^{12}.$$ The bound does not depend on the nodes, however close together they are.
The construction

Let $B(z)=\prod_i(z-x_i)/(1-x_iz)$ be the Blaschke product with the nodes as zeros, and $F=z^{D_*}/B$. On the imaginary axis the logarithmic derivative satisfies $\tfrac{d}{d\log u}\log|B(iu)|\le\sum_i(1-x_i^2)/(1+x_i^2)$, one term per factor. So (67) gives $|B(iu)|\ge u^{D_*}$, and hence $|F(iu)|\le e^2$ on the segment $\Gamma=[-i(1+2/n),\,i(1+2/n)]$ (68).

The required values jump from $c_-=1$ on the left half-disk to $c_+=-5$ on the right. To bridge this, glue the two half-plane functions with a Cauchy integral over $\Gamma$:

$$h_*(z)=c_\pm z^{D_*}-B(z)\,\mathcal C(z)\quad(\pm\operatorname{Re}z\gt0),\qquad\mathcal C(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma\frac{6F(w)}{w-z}\,dw. \tag{69}$$

By the Plemelj jump, $\mathcal C$ jumps by $6F$ across $\Gamma$, and $6BF=6z^{D_*}=(c_--c_+)z^{D_*}$. So the two formulas agree and $h_*$ is holomorphic. Since $B(x_i)=0$, $h_*$ interpolates. On the circle $|B|=1$, so it remains to bound $\mathcal C$. Away from $\pm i$ the distance to $\Gamma$ controls it. Near $\pm i$ the contour is pushed around $z$ by a small rectangle of width $1/n$, where $|F|\le e^{12}$ (70). Maximum modulus then gives the bound on the disk.

5.3Evaluation in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space

Proposition 5.2 (Interpolation estimate). Under (67), $$|\cR_n(x)|\ \le\ (1+K_0n)^n\,\cV(1/x)\prod_i|x_i|^g, \tag{71}$$ where $\cV$ is the absolute Vandermonde. The prefactor is $\exp(O(n\log n))$, uniformly in the nodes.

The point is to avoid dividing by a Vandermonde determinant. When the nodes cluster, that determinant is tiny and its inverse matrix is badly conditioned. The paper instead works in the $n$-dimensional space $\cH_Q=\{v/Q:\deg v\lt n\}$, with $Q(z)=\prod_i(1-x_iz)$, under the Hardy space $H^2$ norm. In this space:

  • the kernels $k_i=1/(1-x_iz)$ form a basis, and $\langle u,k_i\rangle=u(x_i)$, so orthogonal projection $\Pi_Q$ preserves the values at the nodes;
  • the reflection $R(v/Q)=z^{n-1}v(1/z)/Q$ is an isometric involution;
  • $J=\Pi_QM_{h_*}$ has norm at most $\|h_*\|_\infty$, so $L=I+JR$ has $\|L\|\le1+K_0n$.

If $E$ is evaluation at the nodes, then $ELR(v/Q)=\big(\tilde v(x_i)+h_*(x_i)v(x_i)\big)_i$ (72), which are exactly the mixed evaluations. Since $\det E=V(x)$ and $|\det R|=1$, the mixed determinant equals $V(x)\det L$ in absolute value, and $|\det L|\le\|L\|^n$. The Vandermonde cancels algebraically; no inverse appears. This is the kernel and compression viewpoint of Sarason (1967).

Proposition 5.3 (Hadamard estimate). For every list of distinct nonzero reals in $(-1,1)$, $$|\cR_n(x)|\ \le\ (3/2)^n\,n^{n/2}\,2^{-\binom n2}\prod_i(1+x_i^2)^{(n-1)/2}|x_i|^{g-(n-1)}. \tag{73}$$

Expand by choosing a sheet $z_i\in\{x_i,1/x_i\}$ in each column. With $z_i=\tan\phi_i$, the Vandermonde of the $z_i$ becomes $\prod(1+z_i^2)^{(n-1)/2}$ times $2^{-\binom n2}$ times a Vandermonde on the unit circle, and Hadamard bounds that by $n^{n/2}$. The factor $2^{-\binom n2}$ is kept, because on the scale $n^2$ it contributes $-\tfrac12\log2$.

5.4The two principal integrands

Combining (65) with the two estimates gives majorants $\cI_{2,n}$ (interpolation, on the set $\Omega_{2,n}$ where (67) holds) and $\cI_{1,n}$ (Hadamard, on its complement) (74). The index $\kappa\in\{1,2\}$ is the exponent of the $x$-Vandermonde after the substitution $t=2x/(1+x^2)$. Since $d\mu^n\,ds$ has mass $2^n$, and factorials and sheet sums are $\exp(O(n\log n))$,

$$\frac{\log|\Delta_N|}{n^2}-\frac12\log2\ \le\ \max_{\kappa\in\{1,2\}}\ \sup_{\Omega_{\kappa,n}}\Big(\frac{\log\cI_{\kappa,n}}{n^2}-\frac12\log2\Big)+O\Big(\frac{\log(n+2)}n\Big). \tag{75}$$

6A uniform energy bound

The majorants are products over $2n$ points, so their logarithms are energies of charged particles. Each majorant puts $n$ charges $x_i\in[-1,1)$ and $n$ charges $s_j\in(0,1)$ in external fields

$$W_\kappa(x)=(\alpha+2\gamma)\log|x|+2\eta\log(1-x)-\big(\tfrac\kappa2+\alpha+\eta+\gamma\big)\log(1+x^2),\qquad W_s(s)=\beta\log s+\gamma\log(1-s), \tag{76}$$

with pairwise interactions (80)

$$\tfrac\kappa2\log|x-x'|+\tfrac12\log(1-xx')+\log|s-s'|-\log(1-2xs+x^2).$$

The goal is to bound the maximum energy as $n\to\infty$ by one-variable quantities. For a sequence $u=(u_k)$ decaying exponentially, define

$$\|u\|_*^2=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{u_k^2}k,\qquad T(u,x)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{u_kT_k(x)}k,\qquad S(u,x)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{u_kx^k}k. \tag{77}$$
Proposition 6.1. Let $p,v$ be two such sequences, with $\lambda=0$ if $\kappa=2$ and any $\lambda\ge0$ if $\kappa=1$. Then $$\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\text{case }\kappa}\Big(\frac{\log\cI_{\kappa,n}}{n^2}-\frac12\log2\Big)\ \le\ (-1+\alpha+\gamma)\log2+\kappa\|p\|_*^2+\tfrac12\|v\|_*^2+\sup_{x}\big\{W_\kappa+\lambda D-2\kappa T(p,\cdot)-S(v,\cdot)\big\}+\sup_{s}\big\{W_s+2T(v,\cdot)\big\}, \tag{78}$$ where $D(x)=2\gamma-2x^2/(1+x^2)$, $x$ ranges over $[-1,1)\setminus\{0\}$ and $s$ over $(0,1)$.

The mechanism takes three steps.

  1. Chebyshev expansion of the log kernel. On $[-1,1]$ (82), $$\log|u-u'|=-\log2-2\sum_k\frac{T_k(u)\,T_k(u')}k.$$ The other two kernels expand in powers, $\log(1-zz')=-\sum_kz^kz'^k/k$, and in Chebyshev–power products for the cross term. Including the diagonal, the interactions become $-\log2$ minus squares of empirical moments: $-\kappa\sum_kA_k^2/k$ and $-\tfrac12\sum_k(B_k-2C_k)^2/k$ (86)–(87).
  2. Damping. The diagonal terms are infinite, so the expansions are damped: $\tau=1-\varepsilon$, $\rho(x)=1-\varepsilon(1-x)$, $\sigma(s)=1-\varepsilon\sqrt{1-s}$ (83). The scales $1-x$ and $\sqrt{1-s}$ match the cross kernel near the corner $(1,1)$, where $|1-xe^{i\arccos s}|^2=(1-x)^2+2x(1-s)$. Every comparison costs $O(\varepsilon)$, and the diagonal corrections cost $O_\varepsilon(1/n)$, uniformly in the configuration. Limits are taken in the order $n\to\infty$, then $\varepsilon\to0$.
  3. Trial sequences. The inequality $-a^2\le b^2-2ab$, with $b=p_k$ and $b=v_k$, replaces each negative square by an expression that is affine in the empirical measure. The many-particle supremum then splits into one supremum over $x$ and one over $s$. Any choice of $p,v$ gives a valid bound. For $\kappa=1$, failure of (67) means $\langle D(x)\rangle\gt-1/n$, so the multiplier term $\lambda D$ costs nothing.

The bound is sharp when $p_k$ and $v_k$ equal the moments of the equilibrium measures. Then each one-variable function is constant on the support of its charges.

7An exact certificate for the two barriers

Proposition 7.1. For the determinants (7), $$\limsup_{N\to\infty}\Big(\frac{\log|\Delta_N|}{n^2}-\frac12\log2\Big)\ \le\ -2.290939875\ \lt\ -2.2909. \tag{88}$$ The separate bounds for the cases $\kappa=2$ and $\kappa=1$ are $-2.290939875$ and $-2.296789875$.

7.1Exact trial sequences

Each trial sequence is a finite polynomial part plus exponential tails, $u_k=l_k+\sum_zr_zz^k$ (89). For $\kappa=2$ the finite parts have length $10$; the tail bases are real or come in conjugate pairs, such as $0.85$, $0.7i$ and $0.092+0.92i$, with moduli at most $0.94$ for $p$ and $0.984$ for $v$. For $\kappa=1$ there are no tails and $\lambda_1=2.47405979$. All coefficients are integers times $10^{-8}$. Summing the series in closed form gives finite formulas with principal logarithms (90), for example

$$T(u,x)=\sum_{k\le d}\frac{l_kT_k(x)}k-\frac12\sum_zr_z\Log(1-2xz+z^2).$$

This defines the two barrier functions:

$$X_\kappa=W_\kappa+\lambda_\kappa D-2\kappa\,T(p,\cdot)-S(v,\cdot),\qquad Y_\kappa=\beta\log x+\gamma\log(1-x)+2\,T(v,\cdot). \tag{91}$$

7.2Derivative numerators and exhaustive root brackets

A plot is not a proof of a supremum. Multiplying $X_\kappa'$ and $Y_\kappa'$ by explicit positive denominators gives polynomials $A_X,A_Y$ with rational coefficients (93), of degrees $36$ and $24$ for $\kappa=2$ and $13$ and $9$ for $\kappa=1$. Their real roots are the stationary points. On each subinterval $(b,c)$, the substitution $x=(b+ct)/(1+t)$ and Descartes' rule of signs bound the number of roots (94). The paper then lists disjoint brackets $(m,m+2)/10^{10}$ where the numerator changes sign (95). The number of brackets equals the Descartes bound on each subinterval, so each bracket holds exactly one simple root, and there are no others.

7.3Rational logarithms and all candidate values

To evaluate the barriers without floating point, every logarithm is replaced by a rational truncation with an explicit error. Real logs use $\log y=2\operatorname{artanh}\frac{y-1}{y+1}$ with $18$ terms after scaling by powers of $2$ (96). Arguments use a rotation into an octant and $\pi/4=\arctan\frac12+\arctan\frac13$, with $24$ terms (97). Each complex log is then accurate to $10^{-15}$. 7.4From bracket values to global suprema

On every bracket $|X_\kappa'|,|Y_\kappa'|\lt120000$, from the coefficient bounds and the fact that all brackets stay at distance more than $0.0007$ from $0$ and $1$. So moving from a bracket's left end to its stationary point gains less than $120000\cdot2\cdot10^{-10}=0.000024$. The singular endpoints have limit $-\infty$. Since $-1+\alpha+\gamma=-11/16$ and $\log2\gt0.693146$, the two cases give

$$-\tfrac{11}{16}(0.693146)+0.77844-0.98399-1.60890+0.000048=-2.290939875,$$ $$-\tfrac{11}{16}(0.693146)+0.9321-1.3244-1.4280+0.000048=-2.296789875.$$

8Conclusion

Proof of Theorem 1.1. Suppose $G\in\Q$. Then every $\Delta_N$ is rational (Prop. 2.1), and $\Delta_p\neq0$ for all large primes $p$ (Prop. 4.1). Along these primes the product formula and Proposition 3.4 give $\liminf\cL_p\gt-2.29084$. Proposition 7.1 gives $\limsup\cL_N\le-2.290939875\lt-2.2909$ along every sequence. Since $-2.29084\gt-2.2909$, these are incompatible. So $G$ is irrational.

The shape of the argument Under $G\in\Q$, the determinant $\Delta_p$ is a nonzero rational number whose denominator is controlled prime by prime: by a $2$-adic smoothing at $2$, by base-$p$ digit reductions and two layers of column elimination at odd primes, and by the prime number theorem in aggregate. Its size is controlled by an integral with two sheets, a uniform interpolation estimate, a log-gas energy and an exact certificate. The two estimates miss each other by $1.3\times10^{-4}$ in the normalized logarithm. The argument gives no irrationality measure: that would need control of all rational approximations, not just one squeeze.

Hyperbolic volumes. Normalize curvature to $-1$. Agol (2010) proved that the minimum volume of an orientable complete finite-volume hyperbolic 3-manifold with exactly two cusps is $4G=3.6638623767\ldots$. It is attained by the complements of the Whitehead link and the $(-2,3,8)$ pretzel link. These volumes are therefore irrational.

More generally, every orientable arithmetic hyperbolic 3-orbifold defined over $\Q(i)$ has irrational volume. Such a lattice is commensurable with the norm-one group $\Gamma^1(\mathcal O)$ of a maximal order in a quaternion algebra $B/\Q(i)$, and since $\zeta_{\Q(i)}(2)=\zeta(2)L(2,\chi_{-4})=\frac{\pi^2}6G$, the volume formula gives

$$\vol\big(\Gamma^1(\mathcal O)\backslash\Hyp^3\big)=\frac G3\prod_{\mathfrak p\mid\mathfrak D}(N\mathfrak p-1).$$

Commensurable lattices have volumes in rational ratio, so each such volume is a positive rational multiple of $G$. The split case is the Picard orbifold:

$$\vol\big(\PSL_2(\Z[i])\backslash\Hyp^3\big)=\frac G3=0.3053218647\ldots$$
