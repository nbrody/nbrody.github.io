The Riley slice, explained

What the viewer draws, and the mathematics behind each layer.

The groups

For a complex number ρ, Riley's group is generated by two parabolics,

Γρ = ⟨ X, Y ⟩,   X = (1 1; 0 1),   Y = (1 0; ρ 1).

The Riley slice is the set of ρ for which Γρ is discrete and free and its regular set uniformizes a sphere with four punctures (Keen–Series). It is symmetric under ρ ↦ −ρ and ρ ↦ ρ̄, contains every ρ with |ρ| ≥ 4, and its complement — the white lens — is a bounded region around the real segment [−4, 4]. The lens contains the disc |ρ| < 1, where Shimizu's lemma already rules out discreteness. Its boundary is a fractal curve made of cusp groups.

Farey polynomials

Every slope p/q gives a word Wp/q in X and Y — the curve of slope p/q on the four-punctured sphere — and its trace is the Farey polynomial Φp/q(ρ). They start from

Φ0/1 = 2 − ρ,   Φ1/1 = 2 + ρ,   Φ1/0 = 2,

and the trace identities of SL2 give the recursion down the Farey (Stern–Brocot) tree: for Farey neighbours L < R with mediant L ⊕ R,

ΦL⊕R = 8 − ΦL ΦR − ΦR⊖L.

The viewer evaluates them through this recursion, never through expanded coefficients, which are enormous (about 1025 already at moderate depth).

Pleating rays and cusps

The pleating ray of slope p/q is the branch of { ρ : Φp/q(ρ) ∈ (−∞, −2] } lying in the slice. Along it the convex-hull boundary of Γρ is bent only along the curve of slope p/q. Each ray comes in from infinity asymptotic to the direction arg ρ = πp/q and lands on the boundary at the p/q cusp, where Φp/q = −2 and Wp/q becomes parabolic. Keen and Series showed that the rays are disjoint and that the rational ones are dense in the slice. That is why the viewer can draw the boundary as the set of cusps of the traced rays.

Extensions and Heckoid points

Past its cusp each ray continues into the lens along the curve where Φp/q is real and in (−2, 2). There Wp/q is elliptic, and the group is usually not discrete. At the points with Φp/q = −2 cos(π/n), Wp/q has order n and the group is discrete again but not free: these are the Heckoid groups, the beads on the extensions.

Riley's regions

In 1985 Robert Riley published a computer plot of the slice with the exterior coloured into regions. The colours count the faces of a Ford domain — the domain of ⟨X, E⟩, where E(z) = 1/(ρz) swaps the two cusps of Γρ. This is the Ford domain of Γρ taken at both of its cusps. Outside |ρ| = 4 it has one face; each wall crossed inwards adds faces, and the regions shrink and multiply towards the boundary. The viewer computes these domains exactly, region by region along each row. Riley's regions explains the faces, the walls and the palettes in detail, with its own explorer.

The square-root plane

The √ρ plane shows z with ρ = z²: the group ⟨(1 z; 0 1), (1 0; z 1)⟩, which conjugating by diag(√z, 1/√z) turns into Γz². The picture is the pullback of the slice under z ↦ z², a double cover with the symmetry of a square and cusps at ±2 and ±2i. There the first walls of Riley's regions are honest circles: |z| = 2 and |z ∓ 1| = 1, |z ∓ i| = 1.

Using the viewer

  • Plane: switch between ρ and √ρ.
  • Selection: click any point, bead, cusp or dot. The panel reports ρ, √ρ and the Farey polynomial at a cusp or bead. It also gives the region (its face count and the faces of the Ford domain, drawn in the inset), or a proof that the group is not discrete, and the dominant slope. Open in Dirichlet tool sends the group to the Hyperbolic Polyhedron Viewer.
  • Depth: the largest denominators of the rays, dots and extensions. Deeper is finer and slower.
  • Ford regions: show the regions, the first walls, the outermost region; compute the Ford domain at both cusps (Riley's picture) or at ∞ alone; colour by face count with a choice of palettes.
  • Drag to pan, scroll to zoom, double-click to reset.
