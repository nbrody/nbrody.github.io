Understanding the Riley Slice

A visual and mathematical guide to parameter space of Kleinian groups

What is the Riley Slice?

The Riley slice is a 2-dimensional cross-section of Schottky space that parametrizes a special family of Kleinian groups— discrete subgroups of PSL(2,ℂ) acting on hyperbolic 3-space. The term "slice" refers to the fact that we're fixing certain parameters to get a lower-dimensional space that's easier to visualize and study.

Specifically, the Riley slice consists of two-generator groups where one of the generators is parabolic (i.e., has a fixed point at infinity and eigenvalue 1). This constraint reduces the dimension from 4 parameters down to 2, making the space 2-dimensional and thus drawable.

The Mathematical Setup

In the Riley slice, we study groups generated by two matrices:

A = ⎛1 1⎞ (parabolic) ⎝0 1⎠ B = ⎛1 0⎞ (hyperbolic, depends on ρ) ⎝ρ 1⎠

where ρ is a complex parameter. Each point in the complex plane ρ represents a different group Γ(ρ) = ⟨A, B⟩. The group is discrete (and thus geometrically interesting) only for certain regions of ρ.

What Makes it "Discrete"?

A group acting on hyperbolic space is discrete if the action has no accumulation points. Geometrically, this means the orbits of points don't cluster together. Not all values of ρ give discrete groups—we need to check certain algebraic conditions involving trace polynomials.

The Farey Tree and Trace Polynomials

The key tool for understanding the Riley slice is the Farey tree, a binary tree of all rational numbers in lowest terms, ordered so that adjacent fractions p/q and p'/q' satisfy |pq' - p'q| = 1.

Associated to each edge p/q in the Farey tree is a trace polynomial Φ_{p/q}(ρ). These polynomials are computed recursively:

Φ_{p/q}(ρ) = 8 − Φ_L(ρ)·Φ_R(ρ) − Φ_{diff}(ρ)

where L and R are the left and right parents in the Farey tree, and "diff" denotes a specific sibling relationship. The recursion bottoms out at primitive base cases.

Why Trace Polynomials?

The trace of a matrix encodes crucial information about its action. For our group to be discrete, the traces need to avoid certain "bad" regions. Specifically:

  • If |Φ_{p/q}| < 2: The group has additional structure related to that fraction
  • If |Φ_{p/q}| = 2: We're on a boundary between regions (boundary curves)
  • If |Φ_{p/q}| > 2: The group is discrete and "far from trouble"

Interpreting the Visualization

The Gray Region (Complement of the Slice)

The gray region is the complement of the Riley slice — parameters where Γρ is not discrete and free — and its boundary is the famous fractal curve of cusp groups. It is rendered following Elzenaar–Martin–Schillewaert: the Farey polynomials Q_{p/q} = 2 − Φ_{p/q} map the slice into itself, their root sets lie in (and are dense in) the complement, so a point within a small Newton distance |2 − Φ| / |Φ′| of a root is provably outside the slice. This is seeded with the disk |ρ| < 1 (excluded by Shimizu's lemma) and the real segment (−4, 4). As the Farey depth grows, the union of root neighborhoods converges to the full complement and the fractal boundary sharpens. The filigree curves inside are the |Φ| = 2 level sets and the elliptic extension curves.

Colored Regions (Riley-Style Tessellation)

The exterior is tiled into regions on which a single Farey word W_{p/q} is dominant — it has the smallest |Φ_{p/q}| among all Farey words. The dominant word controls the combinatorics of the Dirichlet domain (the fundamental domain for the group's action on hyperbolic space), so each region carries one combinatorial type. This is the modern rendering of Robert Riley's original computer plot, where each printed symbol recorded the combinatorial type of the Ford domain his Poincaré procedure found.

The two coarsest slopes 0/1 and 1/1 own the deep exterior; curvy bands of higher-denominator regions accumulate on the boundary, one lens-shaped region over each cusp. The dark walls mark where dominance passes between Farey-neighbor slopes.

Pleating Rays (Yamashita-Style Lamination)

These curves mark where the trace polynomial is real and ≤ −2: Im(Φ_{p/q}) = 0 and Re(Φ_{p/q}) ≤ −2. Each ray P_{p/q} runs from the p/q cusp on the boundary out to infinity, asymptotic to the direction πp/q; by Keen–Series theory the rays are pairwise disjoint and foliate the exterior. They are drawn as thin uniform ink curves, in the style of Yasushi Yamashita's celebrated pictures of the Riley slice: together the rays laminate the exterior, accumulating in a dense fringe along the boundary.

Geometrically, on these rays, the group has a pleated surface—roughly speaking, a hyperbolic surface glued along certain axes. These are some of the most studied points in the slice because they have additional geometric structure.

Ray Extensions (Fainter Curves)

These show the continuation of each pleating ray past its cusp into the non-discrete region, where Φ_{p/q} is real in (−2, 2) and the Farey word W_{p/q} becomes elliptic. While the groups here aren't discrete (so less geometrically interesting), the extensions illustrate the deeper structure underlying the combinatorics.

Boundary Curves (Dark Lines)

These mark where |Φ_{p/q}| = 2 for various fractions p/q. They are the boundaries between different combinatorial regions, representing transitions in the Dirichlet domain type.

What Do Different Regions Represent?

The visualization shows the Riley slice colored by combinatorial type. Here's what the structure represents:

Outer Layers (Low-Denominator Regions)

The outermost, "coarser" regions correspond to low-denominator Farey fractions like 1/1, 1/2, etc. These regions are "simpler" in that their Dirichlet domains have fewer faces. Groups here have relatively simple structure.

Inner Layers (High-Denominator Regions)

As you approach the boundary of the gray region, you encounter bands of regions with higher-denominator fractions like 3/7, 4/9, etc., one lens-shaped region over each cusp. These correspond to more intricate Dirichlet domains with more faces. This creates the self-similar fractal structure.

The Radial Pattern

The radiating pattern you see (with regions emanating from a central point) follows the Farey tree structure. Moving along different "directions" from the center corresponds to traversing different paths through the tree.

Further Reading

The Riley slice was introduced by Evan James Riley in his study of Kleinian groups. Key mathematical topics:

  • Kleinian groups and hyperbolic geometry
  • Schottky space and deformation theory
  • Farey tessellations and their role in parameter space
  • Pleating coordinates and earthquake theory
  • Dirichlet domains and fundamental domains

Much of the modern understanding comes from work combining computer-assisted exploration with rigorous mathematical analysis.
