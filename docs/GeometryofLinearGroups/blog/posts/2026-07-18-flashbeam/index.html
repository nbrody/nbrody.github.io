The FlashBeam search

July 18, 2026

Here is a problem that comes up constantly when experimenting with groups: we have a finite set of generators — matrices, say — and we want a word in the generators whose product is some target element, or at least comes as close to it as possible. Perhaps we want words representing the identity, or an integral matrix, or an element with a particular trace. The space of words of length $\ell$ grows exponentially in $\ell$, and the interesting words are often hundreds of letters long, so exhaustive search is hopeless almost immediately.

Beam search

The standard heuristic here is a beam search. Choose a score function on words — some measure of how far the product matrix is from the target. Maintain a "beam" of the $W$ best words found so far. At each step, extend every word in the beam by every generator, score all the resulting products, and keep the best $W$ of those as the new beam. This is a breadth-first search that only ever remembers a bounded frontier, so it runs forever in constant memory.

Beam search has two chronic weaknesses. First, it's slow: words grow by one letter per iteration, so a 500-letter solution takes at least 500 iterations. Second, it's greedy: if every single-letter extension of the current beam makes the score worse, the search stalls on a plateau, and a wider beam only postpones the problem.

The FlashBeam variation

FlashBeam modifies the generating set itself as the search runs. Alongside the beam, we maintain a second population: the flash pool, consisting of the $F$ best-scoring words seen at any point so far — not just in the current generation. The expansion step then multiplies beam words not only by the original generators, but by every word in the flash pool, on both the left and the right.

One FlashBeam iteration
  1. Form the expansion pool: the original generators together with the flash pool of the $F$ best words seen so far.
  2. Multiply every beam word by every pool word, on both sides, skipping products that immediately cancel.
  3. Score each product, discard any whose matrix has been visited before, and keep the best $W$ as the new beam.
  4. Merge the new beam into the flash pool, keeping the best $F$ — so good words are promoted into the generating set for every later iteration.

In effect the search builds its own vocabulary. Once it discovers a good block — a word that lands unusually close to the target — that block becomes a macro-generator and gets reused wholesale. Word length can double in a single iteration instead of growing by one, the way repeated squaring reaches $x^{512}$ in nine multiplications rather than 511. And plateaus are less sticky: even when no single letter improves the score, multiplying by a substantial block often does.

Practical notes

  • Deduplication. Hash each product matrix and never revisit a state. Without this, the beam fills up with dozens of spellings of the same element.
  • Free reduction. A word that hits the target should be checked for nontriviality — a word that freely reduces to nothing is the identity for boring reasons. Similarly, skip extensions that immediately cancel against the end of the word.
  • Two knobs. The beam width $W$ controls breadth and the flash size $F$ controls how aggressive the macro-moves are. In practice $W$ in the hundreds with $F$ around a few dozen works well; a huge $F$ makes each iteration quadratically expensive without finding much more.
  • Verify exactly. If the score is computed in floating point, a "solution" is only a candidate: roundoff can manufacture convincing near-misses. Every hit should be re-verified in exact arithmetic before it's believed.

Nothing about the method is specific to matrices — it applies whenever elements can be multiplied, hashed, and scored. Several search tools on this site run FlashBeam live in the browser, with the beam and flash pool visible as they evolve.
