What “nice” means here

                    
A spatial graph is an embedding up to isotopy, so “nice” has to pick a representative. Here it
                        is a low point of an energy that depends only on the shape:

                    
    
                        
  • Self-avoidance. The tangent-point energy Σ ℓIℓJ|T×e|α/|e|β
                            over pairs of pieces: it blows up where two strands meet but vanishes along a straight
                            strand, so strands spread apart evenly.
    • 
                        
  • Smoothness. Bending energy ∫κ² ds along each edge.
    • 
                        
  • Balanced junctions. Edges leave each vertex as evenly spread as possible (the Thomson
                            problem on directions): 120° in a plane at a trivalent vertex, as in a soap film.
    • 
                        
  • Fairness. Edges that a symmetry of the abstract graph could swap get equal length.
                            Without this a θ-curve settles as a flat circle with a diameter.
    • 
                    

                    
Total length is held fixed, so only the shape matters. Minimizers tend to be symmetric:
                        the rigid motions that map the shape to itself are found above and drawn under View — the other
                        sense of “nice”: as many of the graph’s symmetries as possible realized by isometries of ℝ³.

                    
A settled shape is a local minimum. Explore kicks it with random twists of space and
                        keeps the lowest energy it finds.

                    
No bead moves more than 0.45× the current clearance (the least distance between pieces that
                        share no bead) in one step, so pieces never meet and the isotopy class is unchanged — the
                        invariants on the Graph tab confirm it.

Spatial graphs, settled

                
            

            

                
The question

                
A spatial graph is a graph embedded in ℝ³, considered up to isotopy: you may move it
                    around as long as no two of its strands pass through each other. Every such class has
                    infinitely many pictures. Which one is the nice one?

                
An answer

                
Make niceness an energy and let the picture flow downhill inside its class. The energy here
                    rewards four things: strands far apart from one another (a tangent-point repulsion, as in
                    Yu, Schumacher and Crane’s Repulsive Curves), smooth edges (bending), edges leaving
                    each vertex evenly spread (balanced junctions: 120° in a plane at a trivalent vertex, like
                    the films in a soap foam), and edges of similar length. Total length is held fixed, so only the
                    shape matters.

                
“Fairness” matters more than you might guess: with repulsion alone, at fixed length, the θ-curve’s
                    minimizer is a flat circle with a diameter. Asking that edges the graph cannot tell apart have equal
                    length makes three meridians of a sphere the winner.

                
The minimizers are typically symmetric, and that is the second sense of nice: the
                    symmetries of the graph that the picture realizes as rigid motions. The tool finds them (by
                    matching every graph automorphism against the geometry) and names the point group — the
                    standard θ-curve settles to D3h, a trefoil to D3.

                
Why it can’t cheat

                
The flow moves beads along a polygonal model. No bead moves more than 0.45× the current
                    clearance in a step, and two segments that are at least the clearance apart cannot meet while
                    each point of them moves less than half of it. So no step changes the isotopy class — scramble,
                    drag and symmetrize included.

                
Using it

                
    
                    
  • Load Example… for θ-curves, handcuffs, K₄ to K₇, Petersen, knots and links.
    • 
                    
  • Draw a diagram: strands, vertices where strokes end on strands, a click on a crossing to
                        flip it. Or project the current shape into the editor and change a crossing.
    • 
                    
  • Text: vertices, edges through waypoints, closed curves, or named graphs like
                        graph petersen.
    • 
                    
  • Drag a strand in 3D to pull it; Scramble twists the surrounding space and lets it
                        settle again — a good test of whether the nice picture is canonical.
    • 
                    
  • Explore hops between energy basins (kick, settle, keep the lowest): a settled shape is only a
                        local minimum.
    • 
                    
  • Invariants (knot determinants of the cycles, linking numbers of disjoint cycles) are
                        recomputed as it settles, as a check that nothing passed through anything.
